# The Bengal and Assam Jugis and the name "Yogi" in the census, 1872–1931

How and when did the weaving caste called Jugi (Jogi) in Bengal and Assam claim the names Nath and Yogi and a
Brahman origin? How did the census respond? The question tests claim 10 in `../results/literature.md`. That claim
comes from a search snippet attributed to Debnath (2023/24), and the book's text was not seen. It says the caste adopted
"Yogi" around 1911 because "Jugi" was confused with "Jungi", that the 1911 superintendent (O'Malley) did not object
to the spelling, and that in 1931 the caste sought to be returned as Brahman.

## Answer in brief

The campaign is older than the literature's dates and went through three stages. The census accepted the name and
refused the varṇa.

Corrections to the literature's version:
1. **The "Jungi" objection and the spelling campaign date from the 1901 census, not 1911.** The thread and the Brahman
   origin legends are attested in 1891 (Risley).
2. **"Yogi" was formally claimed in 1911 and formally adopted in 1921, in Assam only.** Bengal kept "Jogi and Jugi" in
   1931.
3. **The 1931 Brahman claim was made by the caste's priests, not by the caste.** They had been leaving the caste for
   "Brahman" since 1891 in Sylhet (Assam 1921, citing the Sylhet Deputy Commissioner in 1891). The Brahman claim and
   the Yogi name ran together. Gait (1901) reports the whole caste claiming descent from "Brahman ascetics or Yogis".
   By 1921–31 the priests sought Brahman status, while the caste as a whole took the ascetic name.
4. The O'Malley remark could not be found in the 1911 Bengal report.

## What the case shows about "yoga" and "yogi"

- **The caste revalued "Yogi" itself.** In the 1890s "yogi" or "jogi" meant a mendicant or a low weaving caste. The caste
  turned it into a mark of status: "the spelling Yogi" emphasised "their ascetic origin".
- **The census officials and the caste agreed on the content of the word.** "Yogi" meant a renunciant ascetic and was
  respectable. "Jugi" meant a weaver and was degraded.
- **Gait's definition (1901, para. 787, p. 432)** gives the officials' range: "Yogi or Jogi is a word of much greater
  ambiguity. It means one who practises yoga or 'devotion', but it is used in a very wide sense of any ascetic,
  including even the filth-eating Aghoris. Used more precisely, it indicates a low class of people who make silk thread,
  string ornaments and sell [them]…"
- **The Bengal Jugis never appealed to bodily practice.** They appealed to ascetic descent: Gorakhnāth, the Nāth
  ascetics, Siddhas and Yoginīs, and burial in a seated posture. "Yoga" in their claim is a lineage, not an exercise.

The 1872–1901 Bengal figures are from the 1901 report's table of caste variation since 1872 (`in.ernet.dli.2015.55930`),
for the old province including Bihar and Orissa. They therefore include the Bihar "Jogis", who are mendicants and
thread-makers, not the Bengal weaving caste. The 1921 and 1931 figures are for the smaller Bengal Presidency and are
not comparable with the earlier ones.
