# Literature check: "yoga", "yogi", "Jogi/Jugi" in British India, c. 1870–1930

Checked with web searches on 5 November 2026. Every reference below was searched for.

- **verified** means I saw the bibliographic details in a publisher page, library catalogue, Crossref record or the
  item itself.
- **unverified** means I could not confirm the details.

Most summaries rest on abstracts, tables of contents and reviews rather than on a reading of the full text. Each
entry says which. Briggs (1938) is the exception: I read the relevant pages in the scanned book. Page references are
given only where I saw them.

---

## 1. Short answer

### What is established

1. **Modern yoga studies have shown that "yoga" and "yogi" came apart in the colonial period.**
   - **Respectable yoga** was textual and philosophical yoga: Patañjali, and Vivekananda's *Rāja Yoga* (1896).
     Indian reformers and Western esotericists made it respectable (De Michelis 2004; White 2014).
   - **The living yogi** (haṭha yogī, Nāth or Kānphaṭa ascetic, wandering mendicant, street performer) was lumped
     together with the "fakir". European writers and high-caste Indians treated him with contempt (Singleton 2010,
     ch. 2–3).
   - **Postural yoga as health and exercise** appeared only from the 1920s, with Kuvalayananda and the physical
     culture movement (Alter 2004; Singleton 2010, ch. 4–5).
2. **Historians of asceticism have shown a second change: the yogi stopped being seen as a soldier.**
   - The armed ascetic was a normal part of the military labour market up to about 1800.
   - The Company disarmed and demobilised these ascetics.
   - By the late nineteenth century they appeared in colonial writing mainly as religious mendicants or vagrants
     (Pinch 2006; Farquhar 1925 is an early colonial statement of this).
   - The policing literature documents how mobile groups and ascetics were criminalised (Sinha 2008; Nigam 1990;
     Maclean 2008).
3. **Scholarship on the Nāths and on Jogi castes has shown that "Jogi" in colonial records usually means a caste or a
   householder community, not a practitioner of yoga.**
   - In Bengal and Assam the Jugis were a weaving caste (Ondračka 2022).
   - From the early twentieth century they claimed the names "Yogi" and "Nath" and Brahman status (Bordeaux 2022;
     Debnath 2023, 2024).
   - In north India, Muslim Jogis did not fit colonial religious categories (Ernst 2005; Kumar 2025).

### How colonial reference works have been used

- The glossaries of Risley, Crooke, Rose and Russell & Hira Lal, and the census reports, are **quoted** in this
  literature, but **only selectively**:
  - a definition from Rose;
  - Risley's "weaving caste";
  - Oman's anecdotes;
  - the 1891 census heading "miscellaneous and disreputable vagrants".
- The fullest compilation of census and gazetteer data on yogis is still **Briggs (1938), ch. 1 and 3**:
  - census figures for 1891, 1901, 1911, 1921 and 1931;
  - Bombay, Eastern Bengal and Punjab gazetteers;
  - the glossaries of Rose, Russell, Crooke and Risley.

  Briggs used them as sources of facts about the Kānphaṭas, not as evidence of how the words were used. Later
  scholars (Ernst 2005, p. 38; Stuparich 2016) take their census figures from Briggs.

### The gap

I found **no study** that does any of the following:

- reads the district gazetteers (or the gazetteers together with the glossaries and census reports) systematically
  as evidence for what "yoga", "yogi" or "Jogi" meant;
- counts the terms, or codes their senses, across the corpus;
- compares regions or decades.

The claims in the literature about the colonial "image" of the yogi rest on:

- travellers' accounts;
- popular print, stage performance, postcards and lantern slides (Singleton; Dadswell 2007; Siebenga 2012;
  Deslippe 2022);
- a handful of ethnographic quotations.

The Bengal Jugi studies use census reports and community sources but hardly the district gazetteers. Two searches
came up empty:

- **Projit Bihari Mukharji**: no work on Jugis or Naths found. His caste work concerns race science and blood
  groups.
- **"Kiran Kumar"**: no scholar of that name on this topic found. The nearest match is **Mukesh Kumar** on Muslim
  Jogis (below).

A corpus study of the gazetteers would therefore be new. It would test, with counts, claims that so far rest on a few
quoted passages; Section 3 lists them.

## 2. Annotated bibliography

### A. Modern yoga and its colonial background

**De Michelis, Elizabeth. *A History of Modern Yoga: Patañjali and Western Esotericism*. London: Continuum, 2004.
xvii + 282 pp. (pbk 2005, ISBN 978-0-8264-8772-8).**
- **Status:** verified (CiNii and Wellcome catalogue records).
- **Argument:** Traces "Modern Yoga" to the meeting of Western esotericism with Bengali reform circles (the Brahmo
  Samaj) and to Vivekananda's *Rāja Yoga* (1896).
- **Relevance:** Shows how the reformers framed "yoga" as a rational, psychological and Patañjalian discipline.
  She does not discuss the Jogi castes or colonial ethnography.

**Alter, Joseph S. *Yoga in Modern India: The Body between Science and Philosophy*. Princeton: Princeton University
Press, 2004.**
- **Status:** verified (Princeton UP page and CiNii).
- **Argument:**
  - Centres on Swami Kuvalayananda (Kaivalyadhama, founded 1924) and the attempt to make yoga a science of health.
  - Covers naturopathic yoga after Gandhi, and the RSS (ch. 3–5).
- **Relevance:** Dates the medical and physical-culture sense of "yoga" to the 1920s and after. Not concerned with
  the ascetic Jogi or with colonial ethnography.

**Singleton, Mark. *Yoga Body: The Origins of Modern Posture Practice*. New York: Oxford University Press, 2010.
(ISBN 978-0-19-539534-1 hbk; 978-0-19-539535-8 pbk).**
- **Status:** verified (Wellcome catalogue record and a published table of contents).
- **Chapters:** Ch. 2, "Fakirs, Yogins, Europeans" (p. 35); ch. 3, "Popular Portrayals of the Yogin" (p. 55).
- **Argument of ch. 2–3:**
  - "Yogin" meant in particular the Nāth haṭha yogins, but the word was used loosely for ascetics, magicians and
    street performers.
  - European writers, and high-caste Indians including Vivekananda, despised the haṭha yogin as a mendicant
    "fakir" or trickster.
  - So the founders of modern yoga avoided āsana until the physical-culture synthesis of the 1920s–30s (ch. 4–5).
- **Evidence:** Travellers, missionary and popular print, stage performers, and Oman (1903), rather than official
  records.
- **Basis of this summary:** reviews and the chapter titles. I did not check page-level detail.

**Newcombe, Suzanne. "The Development of Modern Yoga: A Survey of the Field." *Religion Compass* 3.6 (2009):
986–1002. DOI 10.1111/j.1749-8171.2009.00171.x.**
- **Status:** verified (Open University and LSE repository records).
- **Content:** Surveys the scholarship and asks how the split between yoga as "fitness" and yoga as "spirituality"
  arose.
- **Relevance:** A good map of the field, but it does not use colonial administrative sources.
- **Also by Newcombe:** *Yoga in Britain: Stretching Spirituality and Educating Yogis* (Sheffield: Equinox, 2019;
  verified). It covers Britain from 1945 to 1980, after the period of this study.

**Jain, Andrea R. *Selling Yoga: From Counterculture to Pop Culture*. New York: Oxford University Press, 2015.**
- **Status:** verified (Oxford Academic record and the Texas A&M catalogue).
- **Content:**
  - Ch. 1 surveys premodern yoga systems.
  - The rest of the book is about late-twentieth-century consumer culture.
- **Relevance:** Marginal for 1870–1930. I found no chapter on colonial yogis or fakirs.

**van der Veer, Peter. *Imperial Encounters: Religion and Modernity in India and Britain*. Princeton: Princeton
University Press, 2001.**
- **Status:** verified (Princeton UP page; table of contents in CiNii).
- **Relevant chapters:** Ch. 3, "The Spirits of the Age: Spiritualism and Political Radicalism"; ch. 4, "Moral
  Muscle: Masculinity and Its Religious Uses".
- **Argument:** Religion was remade in India and Britain together, through the imperial encounter.
- **Basis of this summary:** chapter titles only. I could not confirm a specific treatment of yogis or fakirs.

**van der Veer, Peter. "Global Breathing: Religious Utopias in India and China." *Anthropological Theory* 7.3 (2007):
315–328.**
- **Status:** verified (MPI publication list and library record).
- **Argument:** Yoga and qigong became global while remaining tied to national identities.
- **Later book:** *The Modern Spirit of Asia: The Spiritual and the Secular in China and India* (Princeton UP, 2014;
  verified). Its ch. 7 compares yoga with qigong.
- **Relevance:** Both treat yoga as part of nationalist "spirituality". Neither uses census or gazetteer evidence.

**White, David Gordon. *Sinister Yogis*. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2009. (ISBN 978-0-226-89513-0).**
- **Status:** verified (UChicago Press and library records).
- **Argument:**
  - Before modern times, "yogi" in Indian sources usually meant a figure of power: a sorcerer, a body-snatcher, a
    mercenary, a spy.
  - It did not usually mean a contemplative.
  - The meditative image is a modern construction.
- **Colonial period:** Ch. 6, "Mughal, Modern, and Postmodern Yogis" (pp. 198 ff.), surveys foreign travellers'
  accounts and carries the story into colonial and modern times.
- **Evidence:** Narrative and travel literature, not administrative records.
- **Critique:** See Mallinson (2014).

**White, David Gordon. *The Yoga Sutra of Patanjali: A Biography*. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2014.**
- **Status:** verified (catalogue records).
- **Content:** Chapters on Colebrooke, Rajendralal Mitra, the Theosophical Society, Vivekananda and Krishnamacharya.
- **Argument:** The *Yoga Sūtra* was largely forgotten in India and was revived, chiefly in English, in the
  nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
- **Relevance:** This is the textual side of the split described in item 1 of Section 1.

**Mallinson, James. "The Yogīs' Latest Trick." *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society* 3rd ser. 24.1 (2014):
165–180. DOI 10.1017/S1356186313000734.**
- **Status:** verified (Crossref).
- **Content:** A review article on *Sinister Yogis*.
- **Argument:**
  - White conflates yoga practice with the powers (siddhis) it was said to produce.
  - White also conflates different ascetic traditions.
- **Relevance:** Needed whenever White's "sinister" thesis is cited.

**Mallinson, James. "Nāth Saṃpradāya." In *Brill's Encyclopedia of Hinduism*, vol. 3, ed. K. A. Jacobsen et al.,
407–428. Leiden: Brill, 2011.**
- **Status:** verified (SOAS repository record).
- **Content:** A standard overview of the order.
- **Points shown in the snippets I saw:**
  - "Nāth" as the name of an order of human ascetics is relatively recent, from about the eighteenth century.
  - The ascetic Nāths are now quite distinct from the householder Nāths.
- **Basis of this summary:** I could not open the full text.

**Mallinson, James. "Yogis in Mughal India." In *Yoga: The Art of Transformation*, ed. Debra Diamond, 35–46.
Washington, DC: Arthur M. Sackler Gallery, Smithsonian Institution, 2013.**
- **Status:** verified (citation found in SOAS listings; I did not see the catalogue itself).
- **Content:** Pictures and texts showing yogis as armed, organised and politically connected before colonial rule.
  This is the baseline against which the colonial "vagrant" image stands out.

**Mallinson, James, and Mark Singleton. *Roots of Yoga*. London: Penguin Classics, 2017. (ISBN 978-0-241-25304-5).**
- **Status:** verified (Penguin UK page).
- **Content:** An anthology of translated premodern yoga texts.
- **Relevance:** Useful for definitions of what yoga meant before the colonial period; not about colonial usage.

### B. Ascetics and Nāths, from colonial writing to modern scholarship

**Oman, John Campbell. *The Mystics, Ascetics, and Saints of India: A Study of Sadhuism, with an Account of the
Yogis, Sanyasis, Bairagis, and Other Strange Hindu Sectarians*. London: T. Fisher Unwin, 1903.**
- **Status:** verified (National Library of Ireland and CRL catalogue records; reviews in the *Spectator* 1903 and
  *JRAS* 1904).
- **Nature of the work:** A primary source rather than scholarship.
  - Oman was a professor in Lahore and wrote from personal observation.
  - His tone is sympathetic but sceptical.
- **Use in later scholarship:** Singleton and Briggs both use him. He is the main English-language source for the
  "sadhu as spectacle" image around 1900.

**Briggs, George Weston. *Gorakhnāth and the Kānphaṭa Yogīs*. The Religious Life of India. Calcutta: Y.M.C.A.
Publishing House; London: Oxford University Press, 1938. xii + 367 pp. (repr. Delhi: Motilal Banarsidass, 1973,
1982).**
- **Status:** verified (title page, JRAS review, and catalogue records). I read the relevant pages in the
  archive.org scan.
- **Ch. 1, "Gorakhnāthīs" (pp. 1–6):**
  - Quotes Rose's *Glossary* (vol. II, p. 389): the word Yogi "has many shades of meaning, from that of saint to
    that of sorcerer or charlatan". In the Punjab the word covers low-caste faqirs and fortune-tellers, "chiefly
    Musalman".
  - Compiles census figures, noting they are "not satisfactory", because mendicants of other orders are included.
  - 1891: "Jogis" were filed under "miscellaneous and disreputable vagrants", 214,546 for India. In the Punjab,
    38,137 were Musalmans.
  - 1901: Jogi (Hindu) 659,891; Jogi (Muhammadan) 43,139; Natha (Hindu) 45,463.
  - 1921: Jogi (Hindu) 629,978; Jogi (Muhammadan) 31,158.
  - 1931: Aghori, Faqir, Jogi, Sadhu and Sannyasi together exceed one million, but the census no longer shows how
    many are Gorakhnāthīs.
- **Ch. 3, "Vows" (pp. 44–61):**
  - Cites Buchanan, Risley, Crooke and the Eastern Bengal District Gazetteer (Webster 1910) on the Bengal Yogi
    (Jugi) castes.
  - These are the two subcastes, Masya and Ekadasi, weavers who were "now taking up agriculture, lime-burning,
    goldsmith's work and subordinate grades in Government service" (pp. 51–52).
- **Overall:** This is the most systematic use of census, gazetteers and glossaries I found. But Briggs uses them as
  sources of facts about the order, not as evidence of changing meanings.

**Farquhar, J. N. "The Fighting Ascetics of India." *Bulletin of the John Rylands Library* 9 (1925): 431–452.**
- **Status:** verified (scan of the article and Who Was Who in Indology).
- **Content:** A colonial-era account of armed sannyāsīs and yogis, based largely on anecdote.
- **Relevance:** Shows that by 1925 the armed ascetic was a historical curiosity to British writers.

**Ghurye, G. S. *Indian Sadhus*. Bombay: Popular Book Depot, 1953.**
- **Status:** verified (CRL catalogue).
- **Content:** An early sociological synthesis of the ascetic orders.
- **Relevance:** Draws on census and colonial ethnography. Just outside the period, but often the channel through
  which colonial categories reached later scholars.

**Pinch, William R. *Warrior Ascetics and Indian Empires*. Cambridge Studies in Indian History and Society 12.
Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2006. (ISBN 978-0-521-85168-8).**
- **Status:** verified (Cambridge front matter, which I read).
- **Chapters:**
  - Ch. 1, "Mughal yogis" (p. 28);
  - ch. 3, "Company gosains" (p. 104);
  - ch. 6, "Indian sadhus" (pp. 231–254);
  - conclusion (p. 255).
- **Argument:**
  - Armed ascetics were part of the military labour market until about 1800.
  - The Company's disarming of them, and nineteenth-century colonial knowledge, turned the ascetic into a purely
    "religious" figure.
  - The warrior ascetic then returned in nationalist memory.
- **Basis of this summary:** the publisher's description and reviews (Curley 2008). I did not see the text of ch. 6.
- **Also by Pinch:** *Peasants and Monks in British India* (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1996). It
  covers Rāmānandī sādhus and caste claims in the census era (title verified; content not checked here).

**Bouillier, Véronique. "Religion Compass: A Survey of Current Researches on India's Nāth Yogīs." *Religion Compass*
7.5 (2013): 157–168. DOI 10.1111/rec3.12041.**
- **Status:** verified (Crossref).
- **Content:** Surveys research on the Nāths.
- **Relevance:** A summary I saw notes that the people counted and described in colonial censuses and gazetteers
  were mostly householder Jogis (bards, temple priests, musicians), not the ascetics. Gazetteer "Jogi" entries
  therefore describe a caste.

**Bouillier, Véronique. *Monastic Wanderers: Nāth Yogī Ascetics in Modern South Asia*. New Delhi: Manohar; London:
Routledge, 2017. (ISBN 978-1-138-09539-7).**
- **Status:** verified (Routledge and bookseller records).
- **Content:** A general survey of the order today, often called the update of Briggs.
- **Relevance:** Mainly ethnographic. Useful for checking which colonial descriptions of monasteries and pilgrimage
  still hold. Earlier work on the Nepal monastery of Caughera: *Ascètes et rois* (Paris: CNRS, 1997).

**Bevilacqua, Daniela, and Eloisa Stuparich, eds. *The Power of the Nath Yogis: Yogic Charisma, Political Influence
and Social Authority*. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 2022. DOI 10.1017/9789048552450.**
- **Status:** verified (Cambridge Core).
- **Content:** Ten chapters. They include the two Bengal chapters below (Ondračka; Bordeaux) and a chapter on
  twentieth-century Gorakhpur temple publications.

**Bevilacqua, Daniela. "Let the Sādhus Talk: Ascetic Understanding of Haṭha Yoga and Yogāsanas." *Religions of South
Asia* 11.2–3 (2018): 182–206. DOI 10.1558/rosa.37023.**
- **Status:** verified (Crossref).
- **Content:** Fieldwork among north Indian sādhus on what they mean by haṭha yoga and āsana.
- **Also by Bevilacqua:** *From Tapas to Modern Yoga: Sādhus' Understanding of Embodied Practices* (Sheffield:
  Equinox, 2024; verified).
- **Relevance:** Contemporary, not colonial. It gives the ascetics' own vocabulary, against which colonial usage can
  be compared.

**Stuparich, Eloisa. "Treading the Frontiers of Hinduness: Yogi Naraharinath in 20th Century Nepal." PhD
dissertation, Cornell University, 2016.**
- **Status:** verified (I read the relevant pages).
- **Content:** Repeats Briggs's 1891 census heading ("miscellaneous and disreputable vagrants"). Quotes Ernst (2005,
  p. 38) on the falling share of Muslims among yogis.
- **Relevance:** An example of how the Briggs figures circulate.

**Ernst, Carl W. "Situating Sufism and Yoga." *Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society* 3rd ser. 15.1 (2005): 15–43.
DOI 10.1017/S1356186304004675.**
- **Status:** verified (Cambridge Core).
- **Argument:** Criticises essentialist accounts that derive Sufism from yoga.
- **Census claim (p. 38, as quoted by Stuparich):**
  - The 1891 census, "which listed all yogis under the category of 'miscellaneous and disreputable vagrants'", gave
    figures showing over 17 per cent of yogis were Muslims.
  - By 1921 the share had fallen to under 5 per cent.
  - Ernst says the figures are hard to interpret.
  - The figures appear to come from Briggs.
- **Reprint:** In *Refractions of Islam in India* (2016).

**Gold, Daniel, and Ann Grodzins Gold. "The Fate of the Householder Nath." *History of Religions* 24.2 (1984):
113–132. DOI 10.1086/462982.**
- **Status:** verified (University of Chicago Press journal page).
- **Content:** An ethnography of householder Nāths (Jogis) in Rajasthan.
- **Relevance:** A caste that keeps the yogi name and some of its ritual roles while living as householders. This is
  the type the gazetteers describe.

**Kumar, Mukesh. "Straddling Across Religious Borders: The Case of the Muslim Jogis." *Journal of the American
Academy of Religion* 93.2 (2025): 208–231. DOI 10.1093/jaarel/lfaf049.**
- **Status:** verified (Oxford Academic).
- **Content:** Fieldwork in Alwar and Mewat, combined with colonial sources.
- **Use of colonial sources:**
  - Quotes Ibbetson and Maclagan (1883) and Rose (1911), who separate the "Jogi proper" from a miscellaneous
    low-caste group, chiefly Muslim.
  - Quotes Crooke, who presents Jogi figures as demons or witches.
  - Argues that this either/or classification could not place a group that was both Jogi and Muslim.
- **Related article:** "Moving beyond faith: Muslim Jogis and religious conversion in north India," *Contemporary
  South Asia* 30.4 (2022): 568–585 (unverified beyond a citation).

**Speziale, Fabrizio. "The Rāwal: a sect of Muslim yogis in colonial India." Lecture, SOAS, 24 April 2024.**
- **Status:** unverified as a publication. I found only the event notice and a related 2025 workshop paper, "From
  criminals to landowners: the Rāwal yogis …, 1860s–1890s".
- **Content:** Uses Punjab police papers of 1869, cited by Rose and Ibbetson.
- **Relevance:** Directly relevant to the Muslim Jogi and Rawal material in Rose. Worth watching for publication.

### C. The Bengal Jugi/Yogi/Nath caste

**Ondračka, Lubomír. "The Search for the Jugi Caste in Pre-Colonial Bengal." In Bevilacqua and Stuparich (2022),
131–162. DOI 10.1017/9789048552450.007.**
- **Status:** verified (Cambridge Core).
- **Argument:**
  - The Jugis are documented as endogamous castes in eastern Bengal only by colonial records.
  - Medieval Sanskrit and Bengali texts never mention them.
  - They probably migrated from western India in the seventeenth century as weavers, their main occupation until
    the nineteenth century.
- **Evidence:** Uses colonial reports (Buchanan, Risley) as the first evidence.
- **Basis of this summary:** the abstract.

**Bordeaux, Joel. "Back When We Were Brahmins: Historical and Caste Critique Among Bengali Householder Nāths." In
Bevilacqua and Stuparich (2022), 163–196.**
- **Status:** verified (Cambridge Core).
- **Content:** The Sanskritising movement among Bengali householder Nāths from the early twentieth century, and the
  "Rudraja Nath Brahmin" claim that Ballāla Sena wrongly expelled them from the varṇa order.
- **Use of colonial sources:** Starts from Risley's "weaving caste" (1891–92) and Buchanan.
- **Basis of this summary:** the abstract. The abstract does not discuss census name changes.

**Debnath, Kunal. *Caste, Marginalisation, and Resistance: The Politics of Identity of the Naths (Yogis) of Bengal
and Assam*. Studies in Critical Social Sciences 274. Leiden: Brill, 2023 [2024 on many records]. DOI
10.1163/9789004689381.**
- **Status:** verified (Crossref chapter records; ÖAW and relbib catalogues).
- **Chapters:**
  - "The Naths (Yogis)" (16–46);
  - "Caste Status and Resistance of the Naths of Bengal" (47–85);
  - "… of Assam" (86–116);
  - later chapters on socio-economic status and politics.
- **Argument (publisher's description):** Since the late nineteenth century an educated elite has claimed Brahman
  status on the basis of an origin myth, while dealing with colonial census enumerations and internal reform.
- **Unchecked details:** A search snippet from Debnath's work (site not recorded; the book's text was not seen) says:
  - the community objected to "Jugi" (confused with "Jungi") and adopted "Yogi" around the 1911 census;
  - the Bengal census superintendent (O'Malley) said there was no objection to the spelling "Yogi";
  - in 1931 the caste sought to be returned as Brahman.

  I could not open the text, so these details are **unchecked**.

**Debnath, Kunal. "An Untold Saga of the Politics of Identity: The Struggle for Recognition of the Naths of Bengal."
*History and Sociology of South Asia* 17.2 (2023): 213–222. DOI 10.1177/22308075231155248.**
- **Status:** verified (Crossref).
- **Content:** A short version of the book's argument. The Naths have contested Brahman control of the priesthood and
  the sacred thread since the mid-nineteenth century.
- **Related article:** Debnath, "Radical-Pragmatic Debate over Reservation: A Study of the Naths of West Bengal and
  Assam," *Explorations* (Indian Sociological Society) 4.1 (2020): 135–148 (unverified beyond the SSRN listing).

### D. Policing, vagrancy, "fakir"

**Nigam, Sanjay. "Disciplining and Policing the 'Criminals by Birth', Part 1: The Making of a Colonial Stereotype –
The Criminal Tribes and Castes of North India." *Indian Economic and Social History Review* 27.2 (1990): 131–164.
Part 2: "The Development of a Disciplinary System, 1871–1900," *IESHR* 27.3 (1990): 257–287.**
- **Status:** verified (RePEc/SAGE records).
- **Content:** The standard account of how the Criminal Tribes Act category was built.
- **Relevance:** Not about yogis specifically. It is the frame for the gazetteer passages that list Jogi groups among
  wandering or criminal castes.

**Sinha, Nitin. "Mobility, Control and Criminality in Early Colonial India, 1760s–1850s." *Indian Economic and Social
History Review* 45.1 (2008): 1–33. DOI 10.1177/001946460704500101.**
- **Status:** verified (RePEc).
- **Content:** How Banjaras, gosains, fakirs and sannyāsīs came to be treated as criminal mobile groups in eastern
  India.
- **Relevance:** Shows the regulation of mendicants before 1871.

**Maclean, Kama. *Pilgrimage and Power: The Kumbh Mela in Allahabad, 1765–1954*. New York: Oxford University Press,
2008.**
- **Status:** verified (NYPL and CiNii).
- **Content:** Includes a chapter, "Controlling Sadhus", on colonial regulation of ascetics at the Mela.
- **Basis of this summary:** chapter title only. I did not see the text.

**Siebenga, Rianne. "Colonial India's 'Fanatical Fakirs' and their Popular Representations." *History and
Anthropology* 23.4 (2012): 445–466. DOI 10.1080/02757206.2013.726716.**
- **Status:** verified (Crossref).
- **Content:** Postcards and magic-lantern lectures about the fakir.
- **Argument:**
  - The images followed a "type" tradition.
  - The lecture texts were rooted in colonial discourse.
  - "Fakir" was the generic label for Hindu and Muslim ascetics alike.

**Dadswell, Sarah. "Jugglers, Fakirs, and Jaduwallahs: Indian Magicians and the British Stage." *New Theatre
Quarterly* 23.1 (2007): 3–24. DOI 10.1017/S0266464X06000595.**
- **Status:** verified (Crossref).
- **Content:** "Fakir" and "yogi" as stage identities in Britain.
- **Relevance:** The performer sense of the words, to compare with district usage.

**Deslippe, Philip. "Fakir: How a Word from India Moved Through American Popular Culture for Nearly a Century." In
*The United States and South Asia from the Age of Empire to Decolonization*, ed. Harald Fischer-Tiné and Nico Slate,
69–82. Amsterdam: Amsterdam University Press, 2022. DOI 10.1017/9789400604421.005.**
- **Status:** verified (Cambridge Core).
- **Content:** Traces the American senses of "fakir" through newspapers and magazines, from the 1860s on:
  - Indian ascetic;
  - stage magician;
  - street seller;
  - con man;
  - visiting yogi or swami.
- **Relevance:** The only study I found that traces a term's change in meaning through a large corpus of print,
  although its method is qualitative, not counting.

**Green, Nile. "Breathing in India, c. 1890." *Modern Asian Studies* 42.2–3 (2008).**
- **Status:** verified (Cambridge Core; I did not confirm the page range).

## 3. Claims that gazetteer evidence could test

1. **The 1891 census put "Jogis" under "miscellaneous and disreputable vagrants".**
   - **Source:** Briggs 1938, pp. 4–5; repeated by Ernst 2005, p. 38, and Stuparich 2016.
   - **Test:** Check the 1891 tables themselves. Then check what heading later censuses and the district gazetteers
     used: Faqir, religious mendicant, caste. Did the "vagrant" filing persist after 1891, or was it a one-census
     artefact that later writers have generalised?
2. **The share of Muslims among yogis fell from over 17% (1891) to under 5% (1921).**
   - **Source:** Ernst 2005, p. 38, apparently from Briggs 1938, pp. 5–6.
   - **Test:** Briggs's 1891 Muslim figure (38,137) is for the Punjab, while his total (214,546) is for India. The
     categories also differ between censuses. Province-level census tables and gazetteer caste tables could show
     whether the fall is real or caused by the change in categories.
3. **"Yogi" ranged "from saint to sorcerer or charlatan". In the Punjab, "Jogi" meant mainly low-caste, chiefly
   Muslim, faqirs and fortune-tellers.**
   - **Source:** Rose, *Glossary* II, p. 389, quoted by Briggs 1938, pp. 1–2; Kumar 2025.
   - **Test:** Code the senses and count them by province and decade.
4. **In nineteenth-century European and elite Indian discourse, the haṭha yogin was merged with the "fakir",
   mendicant and showman, and held in contempt.**
   - **Sources:** Singleton 2010, ch. 2–3; Siebenga 2012; Dadswell 2007.
   - **Tests:**
     - How often do "Jogi" and "fakir" co-occur?
     - Do the district writers use "fakir" for Hindu ascetics?
     - What share of passages are hostile?
   - A mostly neutral district record would qualify the claim that contempt was general. It would point instead to
     a popular and missionary register that differs from the administrative one.
5. **The respectable "yoga" of the period was Patañjali's and Vivekananda's rāja yoga. Haṭha yoga was despised.**
   - **Sources:** De Michelis 2004; Singleton 2010; White 2014.
   - **Test:** Does "yoga" in the gazetteers mean the Patañjali school, and is it set against the living Jogi?
6. **The armed ascetic dropped out of colonial view after the early nineteenth century. By 1900 the ascetic was a
   "religious" figure or a vagrant.**
   - **Sources:** Pinch 2006, introduction and ch. 6; Farquhar 1925.
   - **Test:** Count gazetteer mentions of armed or fighting Jogis, Gosains and Nāgas, and see whether they are past
     tense (history sections) or present tense (population sections).
7. **The "sinister" yogi (sorcerer, magician, possessor of powers) persisted into the colonial period.**
   - **Source:** White 2009, ch. 6; disputed by Mallinson 2014.
   - **Test:** How often do the gazetteers attribute sorcery, exorcism, charms or magical powers to Jogis, compared
     with begging, weaving or other trades?
8. **"Nāth" as the name of the ascetic order is recent, from about the eighteenth century.**
   - **Source:** Mallinson 2011.
   - **Test:** Date the gazetteer uses of "Nath" for the order, against "Kānphaṭa" and "Gorakhnāthī", and against
     "Nath" as a Bengal caste name.
9. **The Bengal Jugis were mainly weavers until the nineteenth century, and then moved into agriculture, trades and
   government service.**
   - **Sources:** Ondračka 2022; Briggs 1938, pp. 51–52, citing the Eastern Bengal District Gazetteer (Webster
     1910).
   - **Test:** Occupational descriptions of Jugis in Hunter's *Statistical Account* (1875–77) and in the Bengal
     District Gazetteers (1908–12).
10. **From about 1900–1911 the Jugis claimed the names "Yogi" and "Nath" (partly to avoid confusion with "Jungi").
    In 1931 they sought to be returned as Brahmans.**
    - **Sources:** Debnath 2023/2024 (from a search snippet, unchecked); Bordeaux 2022.
    - **Test:** When do the gazetteers first record the names "Nath", "Pandit-Nath" or "Yogi" and the status claims?
      Compare with the Bengal and Assam census reports of 1901, 1911, 1921 and 1931.
11. **Colonial classification could not place Muslim Jogis, and split the "Jogi proper" from low, chiefly Muslim
    Jogis.**
    - **Sources:** Kumar 2025; Ernst 2005; Speziale (lecture).
    - **Test:** Collect gazetteer and glossary passages on Muhammadan Jogis and Rawals, and see where they are filed
      (caste table, religion table, criminal or vagrant notes).
12. **Ascetics and mobile mendicants were made a policing problem.**
    - **Sources:** Sinha 2008; Nigam 1990; Maclean 2008.
    - **Test:**
      - Which Jogi groups appear in the police and crime sections of the gazetteers, or in the criminal-tribe
        lists?
      - Is that a regional pattern (for example the Telugu Jogis and the Central Provinces) or a general one?
13. **Yoga as bodily health and exercise appears only from the 1920s.**
    - **Sources:** Alter 2004; Singleton 2010, ch. 4–5.
    - **Test:** Check whether any gazetteer or census text before about 1930 treats āsana or yoga as exercise or
      therapy. Check what "practice of yoga" refers to when it occurs.
