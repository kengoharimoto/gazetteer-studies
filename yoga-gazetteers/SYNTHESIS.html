Yoga, yogis and Jogis in the gazetteers

What did “yoga”, “yogi” and “Jogi” mean to the officials who wrote the gazetteers, caste glossaries and census reports, and how did that differ by region and change over time?

These officials described the districts of British India, its castes, sects and occupations, and their successors did the same after independence. Their volumes have not been read systematically for yoga. We read 359 of them and coded 3,996 passages; 3,101 of them speak of yoga, yogis, Jogis, fakirs, sādhus or the ascetic orders, each with a verbatim quotation.

The answer

  1. Two words for two things. Yoga meant Patañjali’s philosophy, and the rare spelling Yogi mostly an ascetic of the past. Jogi meant living people. The officials knew that Jogi came from Yoga, and then described Jogis who had nothing to do with it.
  2. Who the Jogi was depended on the region. In Bengal, Madras, the Central Provinces and the United Provinces he was mostly a member of a caste, low in rank and doing ordinary work: weaving, farming, catching snakes. In the Punjab, and in Mysore and Kanara, he was mostly an ascetic of the Nāth order. In Bombay he was often a wandering singer or beggar. Where he was a caste, the officials recorded him as they recorded any other caste: by rank and trade.
  3. The change came after 1900. Within the colonial period the kinds of Jogi changed little. Then the Jugi weavers of Bengal and Assam claimed the names Jogi and Yogi (1901–1931). After independence the revised gazetteers seldom mention Jogis, and they treat yoga with respect: as philosophy, through Vivekananda, and from the 1950s as exercise.

Terms

Yogi, Jogi, Jugi
Three spellings of one name. “Yogi” is the Sanskrit form, “Jogi” the vernacular form, “Jugi” the Bengali form. The gazetteers treat “Yogi” and “Jogi” almost as two words: “Yogi” mostly for the past, “Jogi” for living people (section 1).
Nāth, Kānphaṭa
The ascetic order that traces itself to Gorakhnāth. Its members are called Kānphaṭa, “split-eared”, from the large earrings given at initiation.
Fakir, sādhu
General words for a religious mendicant, the first Arabic in origin, the second Indic.
Sannyāsī, gosāīn, bairāgī
Members of the large Hindu ascetic orders, Śaiva (sannyāsīs, often called gosāīns) and Vaiṣṇava (bairāgīs).
1 · Meaning

Two words for two things

The officials defined the word by its origin, then described something else. The left column shows what they said the word meant; the right, whom they actually met.

The word, as defined
In its literal and much-respected sense, Yogi means a follower of Patanjali’s Yoga school who is devoted to divine meditation.Bombay Gazetteer IX-1, 1901
The Jogi is, strictly speaking, a Shaiva, and a follower of the Yoga, or philosophical school of Patanjali.Atkinson, NWP Gazetteer, 1879
The Jogi, as described
Used more precisely, it indicates a low class of people who make silk thread, string ornaments and sell strings to be worn as charms round the neck or waist. Many of them are strolling beggars.Gait, Census of Bengal 1901, para. 787
First are the Jogis proper, a regular religious order of Hindus, which includes both the Aughar Jogis and the Kanphatta Jogi ascetics.Rose, Glossary II, 1911. His second class is the low-caste Jogis, whom he calls rascally beggars.

The same two steps recur from Atkinson (1879), whose Jogi entry goes on to describe mountebanks, to the Bombay census of 1911 and Enthoven (1922): “The term Jogi is derived from the Sanskrit yoga, union, and means a follower of the Yoga or Patanjal school of philosophy”, followed by an account of a caste.

“Yogi” is rare and mostly of the past: about 120 colonial hits against 1,270 for “Jogi”. Of the 46 colonial passages that use it, 25 refer to a holy man of antiquity, legend or sculpture, such as the cave-dwellers of Kanheri or Śiva “the great Yogi”.

Yoga as a practice is rare too, and means meditation or trance: Dayānanda’s yoga vidyā, prāṇāyāma in the Brahman’s daily rites. No colonial passage treats posture as exercise.

The census headings blurred this. Enumerators used “Goshain, Bairagi, Jogi, Saniyasi” interchangeably (NWP and Oudh, 1891), census tables filed Jogis under “Faqir”, the general heading for mendicants, and Gait called the word “used in a very wide sense of any ascetic” (Bengal 1901). Yet in the same report he says that, “used more precisely”, it means “a low class of people who make silk thread”. The headings put Jogis among the ascetics; the descriptions mostly show a caste.

3 · Change

The change came after 1900

The kinds of Jogi by period

The Jugis of Bengal and Assam claim the name

The Jugis were a weaving caste of low rank. From the 1890s they claimed descent from Nāth ascetics and Brahmans, and from 1901 they asked the census to record them as Jogi, later as Yogi. The census reports record each step:

1872–1877
Counted with the Patuas; in Hunter’s Statistical Account a despised weaving caste, “Jogi or Jugi”.
1891
Risley records legends of descent from Siddhas and Yoginīs, or from Nātha ascetics and Brahman widows. “In the Presidency districts the Jugis are assuming the sacred thread en masse.”
1901
“The Jogis strongly objected to the ordinary spelling of their caste name (Jugi) which is popularly derived from Jungi.” They call themselves Nath and their wives Debi. Gait adopts “Jogi” but rejects the Brahman claim.Census of Bengal 1901, para. 613, p. 381
1910–1911
District gazetteers: “Most Jugis call themselves Nath”; “the Jugis, or Jogis as they prefer to be called”. The 1911 census lists the name “Yogi” among the names claimed.Census of Bengal 1911, para. 828, p. 442
1921
Bengal: a Brahman enumerator “would rather cut off his hand than write down a Jugi as Jogi and his wife with the title of Debya like a Brahman woman”. Assam adopts Yogi as the caste name; its leaders chose the spelling “to emphasise their ascetic origin”.Census of Bengal 1921, p. 349; Census of Assam 1921, ch. XI
1931
The caste claims “Yogi” again. Its priests ask to be recorded “merely as Brahmans”, and are refused. Bengal keeps the heading “Jogi and Jugi” (384,634 persons).Census of Bengal 1931, pp. 428, 467–468

The claim rested on descent, never on practice. (Debnath, whom we know only from a snippet, dates the change of name to about 1911; the census shows it began in 1901.)

After independence

The revised gazetteers of 1954–1989 seldom mention Jogis. Yoga appears in three ways, always with respect:

  • Sanskrit learning: “Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vedanta” in college curricula, Patañjali manuscripts in private libraries.
  • Vivekananda, Aurobindo and the Ramakrishna Mission. One colonial volume already has this: Howrah (1909) says Vivekananda “travelled in America, expounding the doctrines of yoga and Vedântism”.
  • Exercise, late and in few places: the “Physical Culture and Yoga School” at Lonavla (Poona 1954); “Hath-yogi: a person following Yoga as a means of physical cultures” (Alwar glossary, 1968).
The historians

The gazetteers and the history of modern yoga

Historians of modern yoga describe a split in the colonial period: a respectable yoga rebuilt from Patañjali’s text (De Michelis 2004; White 2014), and a living yogi lumped with the fakir, known as a showman and despised (Singleton 2010, ch. 2–3). Others see him differently: as a sinister figure of power long before colonial rule (White 2009), as a soldier disarmed by the Company (Pinch 2006), or, in Bengal, as a caste of weavers (Ondračka 2022; Bordeaux 2022). Their evidence is mainly travel writing, missionary tracts and the reformers’ own books. No published study has used the officials’ records systematically on these questions; Briggs (1938) comes closest.

The historians’ “yogi” is a wider category than the officials’ word. The historians did not look only for the word: they took in whoever they judged to be a yogi or to practise yoga, whatever the source called him. Singleton’s yogi includes haṭha yogins, Nāth ascetics, wandering mendicants and street performers, many of them called fakirs in his sources; Pinch’s warrior ascetics are gosāīns, sannyāsīs and nāgās. This study starts from the words “yoga”, “yogi” and “Jogi” as the officials used them. A fakir on a bed of nails is a yogi for the historians, but not a Jogi in the gazetteers unless the writer calls him one. So, to look for the historians’ yogi under other names, we also coded about 1,250 passages on fakirs and sādhus and about 880 on sannyāsīs, gosāīns and bairāgīs.

Contempt

Two historians describe a despised yogi. For Singleton (2010) Europeans and high-caste Indians held the living yogi in contempt in the colonial period; for White (2009) he had been a sinister figure of power long before. If the officials shared that view, it should show in their own judgements. Judgement was coded from the writer’s own words. Of 737 colonial passages on yoga, yogis and Jogis, 94% pass no judgement and 27 are negative; of the fakir and sādhu passages 5% are negative, of those on the orders 3%. The contempt on Jogis comes mostly from the literature on “criminal” castes (Thurston on the Telugu Jogis, 1909; the Central Provinces gazetteers, 1907 and 1909) and from Rose’s Punjab glossary. One scholar’s sentence, L. D. Barnett’s, was quoted twice: “the Yoga system in its practical side is largely based upon vulgar ideas of magic and Shamanism, and hence many of its professors have always been vulgar charlatans” (Damoh 1911; reprinted in the revised Amravati gazetteer, 1968).

The showman

Singleton (2010) describes the living yogi as lumped with the fakir and known as a showman, by his feats: the raised arm, the bed of nails. If the officials saw him so, they should describe such feats. Feats and practices are described in only 5% of the fakir and sādhu passages and 10% of those on the orders, mostly Sufi exercises or saints’ legends. A search of all the passages for the classic feats finds eight: arms held raised over the head (Nasik 1883), standing on one leg by the fire (Rose 1914; Central Provinces 1909), the five fires (Russell and Hira Lal 1916), a bed of sharp thorns (Thurston 1909), lying on sharp points (Adams, Western Rajputana States, 1899), a photograph of an anchorite on iron nails (Russell and Hira Lal 1916), and a saint said to have hung head downwards (Central Provinces 1906, a legend). They are reported plainly, without wonder or scorn. The census did file Jogis under “Faqir”, but “fakir” in these volumes is simply the word for a mendicant: more than two-fifths of the colonial fakir passages (427 of 987) name no religion, and where one is named, about a quarter are Hindu or Sikh. Nor were the large Hindu orders tied to yoga: only 45 of 883 passages on sannyāsīs, gosāīns and bairāgīs link them with yoga or Jogis, almost always through census classification.

The soldier

Pinch (2006) finds that armed ascetics, ordinary soldiers until about 1800, were disarmed by the Company and became mendicants. If so, the officials should speak of them only as a thing of the past. Among the passages on the orders, 72 speak of armed ascetics in the past tense and 2 in the present: the Hardwar battles of 1760 and 1796, the Sannyāsī raids in Bengal in the 1780s, “Formerly Gosavis took service as soldiers” (Satara 1885).

Sources and method

  • Volumes. 306 colonial volumes (1866–1935): district gazetteers; the caste glossaries of Risley, Rose, Russell and Hira Lal, Thurston and Enthoven; census reports. They cover Bombay and Sind, Madras, the United Provinces, Bengal–Bihar–Orissa, the Punjab, the Central Provinces, Mysore and Kanara, Rajputana and Baluchistan. Also 53 revised state gazetteers (1954–1989).
  • Coding. Language-model coders followed written rules; every quotation was checked against the scan by script. Random samples (20, 16 and 14 passages) were reread by hand, with one minor miscode in each of the last two.
  • Counts are of passages, not people or opinions. One long glossary article (Rose, Thurston, Russell) yields many passages.
  • Judgement was coded from the writer’s own words; opinions he reports from others count as neutral.
  • Coverage. The search terms are English spellings, so ascetics described without these words are missed. Madras is thin before 1935; Hyderabad and Kashmir are not covered.
