Yoga, yogis and Jogis in the gazetteers

Historians of modern yoga describe a colonial yogi who was despised as a fakir and a showman, and a respectable “yoga” that was rebuilt from texts by Vivekananda and others. Their evidence comes mostly from travel writing, missionary tracts and the press. How did the officials who governed India’s districts write about yoga and yogis?

Terms

Yoga
In these texts, mainly the philosophical school (darśana) of Patañjali’s Yoga-sūtra, or meditation and trance.
Yogi, Jogi, Jugi
Three spellings of one word. “Yogi” is the Sanskrit form. “Jogi” is the vernacular form the gazetteers use for living people. “Jugi” is the Bengali form, used above all for a weaving caste of eastern Bengal and Assam.
Nāth, Kānphaṭa
The ascetic order that traces itself to Gorakhnāth. Its members are “Kānphaṭa” (split-eared) from the large earrings given at initiation.
Fakir, sādhu
General words for a religious mendicant. “Fakir” is Arabic in origin; “sādhu” is Indic.
Sannyāsī, gosāīn, bairāgī
Members of the large Hindu ascetic orders: Śaiva renunciants (sannyāsīs, often called gosāīns) and Vaiṣṇava renunciants (bairāgīs). The companion Śaṅkara study is about the first.

Sources

The district gazetteers, the caste glossaries (Risley, Rose, Russell and Hira Lal, Thurston, Enthoven) and the census reports were written by officials, mostly British, whose task was to classify people by caste, sect, occupation and land. The revised state gazetteers of 1954–1989 continued the series after independence, written by Indian officials.

We read 359 volumes: 306 colonial ones (1866–1935) covering Bombay and Sind, Madras, the United Provinces, Bengal–Bihar–Orissa, the Punjab, the Central Provinces, Mysore and Kanara, Rajputana and Baluchistan, and 53 revised gazetteers. We coded 3,613 passages for what the word referred to and for the writer’s own judgement. Every coding cites a verbatim quotation.

The answer

  1. The officials split “yogi” into two words. Yoga was Patañjali’s philosophy, learned and respectable. Jogi named living people: a caste, members of the Nāth order, a census heading. The officials kept the two apart when they defined the words, and mixed them when they counted people.
  2. They wrote about Jogis as administrators, not as critics. Their judgement is neutral in 94% of passages. The fakir-showman of the travel books, with his raised arm and bed of nails, hardly appears even under other words. Contempt is confined to the criminal-caste literature and a few quoted authorities. An officer recording castes, land and revenue had little use for the exotic ascetic.
  3. The prestige of the word changed hands later. Between 1901 and 1931 the Jugi weavers of Bengal and Assam claimed the names Jogi and Yogi as a sign of ascetic descent, against the resistance of census officials. After independence the revised gazetteers treat yoga with respect, but mostly as philosophy and spirituality. Yoga as exercise first appears in the 1950s and 1960s.
Evidence 1 · What the words meant

Two words

When the officials defined the words, they kept them apart, and several say outright that the living Jogi bears the name of a philosophy he no longer represents.

Yoga and yogi, as defined
In its literal and much-respected sense, Yogi means a follower of Patanjali’s Yoga school who is devoted to divine meditation.Bombay Gazetteer IX-1, 1901
The Jogi is, strictly speaking, a Shaiva, and a follower of the Yoga, or philosophical school of Patanjali.Atkinson, NWP Gazetteer, 1879
Jogi, as described
Used more precisely, it indicates a low class of people who make silk thread, string ornaments and sell strings to be worn as charms round the neck or waist. Many of them are strolling beggars.Gait, Census of Bengal 1901, para. 787
First are the Jogis proper, a regular religious order of Hindus, which includes both the Aughar Jogis and the Kanphatta Jogi ascetics.Rose, Glossary II, 1911. His second class is the low-caste Jogis, whom he calls rascally beggars.

The same pattern runs from Atkinson (1879), whose Jogi entry goes on to describe mountebanks, to the Bombay census of 1911 and Enthoven (1922): “The term Jogi is derived from the Sanskrit yoga, union, and means a follower of the Yoga or Patanjal school of philosophy”, followed in both by an account of a caste.

When the officials counted people, the line blurred. Gait admits that “Yogi or Jogi is a word of much greater ambiguity … used in a very wide sense of any ascetic”. The NWP and Oudh census of 1891 found that “the words Goshain, Bairagi, Jogi, Saniyasi were apparently used by a considerable proportion of the enumerators as interchangeable”. The census filed Jogis under “Faqir” and merged them with “Rawal” and “Nath”.

Yoga as something practised in the present is rare, and it means meditation or trance: Dayānanda’s yoga vidyā, a Bairāgī meditating on his yoga-daṇḍa, prāṇāyāma in the Brahman’s daily sandhyā. No colonial passage treats posture as exercise.

Evidence 2 · Who the Jogi was

Caste, order, saint or beggar

The chart counts only the passages about Jogis (Jogi, Jugi, Kānphaṭa, Gorakhnāthī), by what each passage says the Jogi was.

Region matters more than date. Before 1935 the Jogi was mainly a caste in Bengal, Madras, the Central Provinces and the UP, and mainly a Nāth ascetic or a saint in the Punjab (home of the Tilla monastery) and in Mysore and Kanara. In Bombay he was as often a beggar or performer. In the Punjab census of 1891, about half of the heading “Jogi & Rawal” was Muslim.

Evidence 3 · How they were judged

Neutral, with three exceptions

Of 737 colonial passages on yoga, yogis and Jogis, the writer’s own judgement is negative in 27. These come from three places:

  • The criminal-caste literature: Thurston on the Telugu Jogis (1909), the Central Provinces gazetteers (1907, 1909), the Etah police circle (1911).
  • Rose’s Punjab glossary, on the Rawals and Muhammadan Jogis.
  • One authority, quoted twice. L. D. Barnett wrote that “the Yoga system in its practical side is largely based upon vulgar ideas of magic and Shamanism, and hence many of its professors have always been vulgar charlatans”. The Damoh gazetteer quoted it in 1911, and the revised Amravati gazetteer reprinted it unchanged in 1968.
Evidence 4 · Under other words

Fakirs, sādhus and the ascetic orders

The contempt and the spectacle that the literature describes might have been recorded under other words. To test this we also coded about 1,250 passages on fakirs and sādhus and about 880 on sannyāsīs, gosāīns and bairāgīs.

  • The judgement is just as neutral. Negative in 5% of fakir and sādhu passages and 3% of passages on the orders.
  • Practice is rarely described. Only 5% of fakir and sādhu passages and 10% of passages on the orders describe any practice, and most of these are Sufi exercises (dhikr, the forty-day chilla, ecstatic ḥāl) or saints’ legends. The bed of points appears once, in a medical officer’s neutral account of Dādūpanthī sādhus (Adams, Western Rajputana States, 1899).
  • “Fakir” was not a Muslim word. Where a religion is stated, Hindu or Sikh ascetics are 29% of “fakir” passages in 1866–1890 and 24% in 1891–1935. Half of all colonial “fakir” passages name no religion: fakirs are simply the mendicants who are fed, endowed or counted. After 1954, “sādhu” replaces “fakir” for Hindu ascetics.
  • The orders are seldom connected with yoga. Only 45 of 883 passages on sannyāsīs, gosāīns and bairāgīs link them with yoga or Jogis, and almost always through census classification, not practice. The orders appear as institutions and estates.
Evidence 5 · Change

The Jugis who became Yogis, and yoga after independence

The weaving caste called Jugi in eastern Bengal and Assam used the officials’ split from below. If a Jugi was a low weaver and a Yogi a renunciant of Brahman or Nāth descent, the caste wanted to be recorded as the second. The census reports record each step.

1872–1877
Counted with the Patuas; in Hunter’s Statistical Account a despised weaving caste, “Jogi or Jugi”.
1891
Risley records legends of descent from Siddhas and Yoginīs, or from Nātha ascetics and Brahman widows. “In the Presidency districts the Jugis are assuming the sacred thread en masse.”
1901
“The Jogis strongly objected to the ordinary spelling of their caste name (Jugi) which is popularly derived from Jungi.” They call themselves Nath and their wives Debi. Gait adopts “Jogi” but rejects the Brahman claim.Census of Bengal 1901, para. 613, p. 381
1910–1911
District gazetteers: “Most Jugis call themselves Nath”; “the Jugis, or Jogis as they prefer to be called”. The 1911 census lists the name “Yogi” among the names claimed.Census of Bengal 1911, para. 828, p. 442
1921
Bengal: a Brahman enumerator “would rather cut off his hand than write down a Jugi as Jogi and his wife with the title of Debya like a Brahman woman”. Assam adopts Yogi as the caste name; its leaders chose the spelling “to emphasise their ascetic origin”.Census of Bengal 1921, p. 349; Census of Assam 1921, ch. XI
1931
The caste claims “Yogi” again. Its priests ask to be recorded “merely as Brahmans”, and are refused. Bengal keeps the heading “Jogi and Jugi” (384,634 persons).Census of Bengal 1931, pp. 428, 467–468

The claim rested on lineage (Gorakhnāth, the Nāth ascetics, burial in a seated posture), never on bodily practice. It is a regional case: it shows how the prestige of the word was used, not where that prestige came from.

The revised gazetteers of 1954–1989 rarely mention Jogis. They show where the prestige came from, in three ways:

  • Sanskrit learning: “Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vedanta” in college curricula, and Patañjali manuscripts in private libraries.
  • Vivekananda, Aurobindo and the Ramakrishna Mission, always treated with respect. One colonial gazetteer already has this: Howrah (1909), where the Mission had its headquarters, says Vivekananda “travelled in America, expounding the doctrines of yoga and Vedântism”.
  • Exercise, late and in few places: Kaivalyadhāma’s “Physical Culture and Yoga School” at Lonavla (Poona 1954). The Alwar glossary (1968) defines “Hath-yogi” as “a person following Yoga as a means of physical cultures”.

Against the literature

No published study has used the gazetteers, glossaries and census systematically on this question; Briggs (1938) comes closest. The corpus tests several claims made in the literature:

Method and caveats

  • Coding. Language-model coders followed written rules. Every quotation was checked verbatim against the scan by script. Random samples (20, 16 and 14 passages) were reread by hand, with one minor miscode in each of the last two.
  • Counts are of passages, not opinions. One long glossary article (Rose, Thurston, Russell) yields many passages, and these weigh heavily in 1891–1935.
  • Judgement was coded from the writer’s own wording; opinions reported from others count as neutral. Religion, and links to yoga, were coded only where stated, so the Hindu share of “fakir” is a lower bound.
  • Coverage. The search terms are English spellings, so ascetics described without any of these words are missed. Madras is thin before 1935; Hyderabad and Kashmir are not covered.
