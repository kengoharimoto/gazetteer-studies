Yoga, yogis and Jogis in the gazetteers

Historians of modern yoga describe a colonial yogi who was despised, a fakir and a showman, and a respectable “yoga” that was rebuilt from texts. Their evidence comes mostly from travel writing, missionary tracts and the press. This study reads a different kind of source: the administrative record that district officials wrote about the people they governed.

Sources

The district gazetteers, the caste glossaries (Risley, Rose, Russell and Hira Lal, Thurston, Enthoven) and the census reports were written by officials, mostly British, who collected information district by district. Their task was to classify people: by caste, sect, occupation and land. The revised state gazetteers of 1954–1989 continued the series after independence, written by Indian officials.

We searched about 350 volumes from nine regions for yoga, yogi, Jogi, Jugi, Kānphaṭa, Gorakhnāth and practice terms, and then for fakir, sādhu, sannyāsī, gosāīn and bairāgī. 3,558 passages were coded for what the word referred to and for the writer’s own judgement, and each coding cites a verbatim quotation. Details of the method are at the end of the page.

The answer

  1. The record split “yogi” into two words. Yoga was Patañjali’s philosophical school, learned and respectable. Jogi named living people: a caste, a Nāth order, a census heading. The officials drew this line themselves when they defined the words, and blurred it when they counted people.
  2. The record is taxonomic, not hostile. The writer’s own judgement is neutral in 94% of colonial passages. The fakir-showman of the travel books, with his raised arm and bed of nails, hardly appears. Contempt is confined to the criminal-caste literature and a few quoted authorities.
  3. The split ran the same way for everyone. The Jugi weavers of Bengal and Assam used it from below: between 1891 and 1931 they claimed the names Nath, Jogi and finally Yogi, as a sign of ascetic descent. After independence the revised gazetteers keep yoga as philosophy and spirituality. Yoga as exercise appears only in the 1950s and 1960s.

Two words

When officials defined the words, they kept them apart. Several say outright that the living Jogi bears the name of a philosophy he no longer represents.

Yoga: the school
In its literal and much-respected sense, Yogi means a follower of Patanjali’s Yoga school who is devoted to divine meditation.Bombay Gazetteer IX-1, 1901
The term Jogi is derived from the Sanskrit yoga, union, and means a follower of the Yoga or Patanjal school of philosophy.Bombay Census 1911; Enthoven, Tribes and Castes of Bombay II, 1922
Jogi: the people
Used more precisely, it indicates a low class of people who make silk thread, string ornaments and sell strings to be worn as charms round the neck or waist. Many of them are strolling beggars.Gait, Census of Bengal 1901, para. 787
First are the Jogis proper, a regular religious order of Hindus, which includes both the Aughar Jogis and the Kanphatta Jogi ascetics.Rose, Glossary II, 1911. His second class is the low-caste Jogis, whom he calls rascally beggars.

Atkinson’s NWP Gazetteer (1879) opens its Jogi entry the same way: “The Jogi is, strictly speaking, a Shaiva, and a follower of the Yoga, or philosophical school of Patanjali”. The same entry then describes Jogis as mountebanks.

When officials counted people, the line blurred. Gait admits that “Yogi or Jogi is a word of much greater ambiguity … used in a very wide sense of any ascetic”. The NWP and Oudh census of 1891 found that “the words Goshain, Bairagi, Jogi, Saniyasi were apparently used by a considerable proportion of the enumerators as interchangeable”. The census filed Jogis under “Faqir” and merged them with “Rawal” and “Nath”. The distinction was clear in definition and muddled in classification.

Yoga as something practised in the present is rare and means meditation or trance: Dayānanda’s yoga vidyā, the Bairāgī’s meditation on his yoga-daṇḍa, prāṇāyāma in the Brahman’s sandhyā. No colonial passage treats posture as exercise.

What “Jogi” named

The chart counts only the passages on Jogis (Jogi, Jugi, Kānphaṭa, Gorakhnāthī). It shows what each passage says the Jogi was.

The answer depends on region more than on date. Before 1935 the Jogi was mainly a caste in Bengal, Madras, the Central Provinces and the UP. He was mainly a Nāth ascetic or a saint in the Punjab, home of the Tilla monastery, and in Mysore and Kanara. In Bombay he was as often a beggar or performer. In the Punjab, about half of the census heading “Jogi & Rawal” was Muslim in 1891.

How they were judged

Of 735 colonial passages on yoga and Jogis, the writer’s own judgement is negative in 27. These cluster in three places:

  • The criminal-caste literature: Thurston on the Telugu Jogis (1909), the Central Provinces gazetteers (1907, 1909), the Etah police circle (1911).
  • Rose’s Punjab glossary, on the Rawals and Muhammadan Jogis.
  • One authority, quoted twice. L. D. Barnett wrote that “the Yoga system in its practical side is largely based upon vulgar ideas of magic and Shamanism, and hence many of its professors have always been vulgar charlatans”. The Damoh gazetteer quoted it in 1911, and the revised Amravati gazetteer reprinted it unchanged in 1968.

Contempt and spectacle might have been recorded under other words, so we coded those too: about 1,250 fakir and sādhu passages and about 880 sannyāsī, gosāīn and bairāgī passages. They show the same thing.

  • Judgement is neutral there too. Negative in 5% of fakir and sādhu passages and 3% of order passages.
  • Practice is rarely described. Only 5% of fakir and sādhu passages and 10% of order passages describe any practice. Most of these are Sufi exercises (dhikr, the forty-day chilla, ecstatic ḥāl) or saints’ legends. The bed of points appears once, in a medical officer’s neutral account of Dādūpanthī sādhus (Adams, Western Rajputana States, 1899).
  • “Fakir” was not a Muslim word. Where a religion is stated, Hindu or Sikh ascetics are 29% of “fakir” passages in 1866–1890 and 24% in 1891–1935. Half of all colonial “fakir” passages name no religion. Fakirs are simply the mendicants who are fed, endowed or counted. After 1954 “sādhu” replaces “fakir” for Hindu ascetics.
  • The orders are institutions and estates. A fifth of sannyāsī, gosāīn and bairāgī passages in 1891–1935 describe married householders: zamindars, money-lenders, an Atīth caste. Only 45 of 883 connect the orders with yoga or Jogis, nearly always through census classification. Armed ascetics appear only as history (72 passages in the past tense, 2 in the present).

The reason is the kind of writing. A district officer recording castes, land and revenue had little use for the exotic ascetic of travel writing. He recorded who the Jogis were, how many there were, what they owned and how they married. Where he passed judgement, it was usually on crime or on lapsed celibacy, not on yogic practice.

Claiming the name

The weaving caste called Jugi or Jogi in eastern Bengal and Assam turned the split to its own advantage. If Jugi was a low weaver and Yogi a renunciant of Brahman or Nāth descent, the caste wanted to be recorded as the second. The census reports record each step.

1872–1877
Counted with the Patuas; in Hunter’s Statistical Account a despised weaving caste, “Jogi or Jugi”.
1891
Risley records legends of descent from Siddhas and Yoginīs, or from Nātha ascetics and Brahman widows. “In the Presidency districts the Jugis are assuming the sacred thread en masse.”
1901
“The Jogis strongly objected to the ordinary spelling of their caste name (Jugi) which is popularly derived from Jungi.” They call themselves Nath and their wives Debi. Gait adopts “Jogi” but rejects the Brahman claim.Census of Bengal 1901, para. 613, p. 381
1910–1911
District gazetteers: “Most Jugis call themselves Nath”; “the Jugis, or Jogis as they prefer to be called”. The 1911 census lists the name “Yogi” among the names claimed.Census of Bengal 1911, para. 828, p. 442
1921
Bengal: a Brahman enumerator “would rather cut off his hand than write down a Jugi as Jogi and his wife with the title of Debya like a Brahman woman”. Assam adopts Yogi as the caste name. Its leaders chose the spelling “to emphasise their ascetic origin”.Census of Bengal 1921, p. 349; Census of Assam 1921, ch. XI
1931
The caste claims “Yogi” again. Its priests ask to be recorded “merely as Brahmans”, and are refused. Bengal keeps the heading “Jogi and Jugi” (384,634 persons).Census of Bengal 1931, pp. 428, 467–468

The claim rested on lineage (Gorakhnāth, the Nāth ascetics, burial in a seated posture), never on bodily practice. It is a regional case. It shows how the word’s prestige was used, not where that prestige came from.

After independence

The revised gazetteers (1954–1989) rarely mention Jogis. Yoga appears in three ways:

  • In Sanskrit learning: “Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vedanta” in college curricula, and Patañjali manuscripts in private libraries.
  • Through Vivekananda, Aurobindo and the Ramakrishna Mission, always respectfully. One colonial gazetteer already has this: Howrah, 1909, where the Mission had its headquarters, says Vivekananda “travelled in America, expounding the doctrines of yoga and Vedântism”.
  • As exercise, late and in few places: Kaivalyadhāma’s “Physical Culture and Yoga School” at Lonavla (Poona 1954). The Alwar glossary (1968) defines “Hath-yogi” as “a person following Yoga as a means of physical cultures”.

Against the literature

No published study has used the gazetteers, glossaries and census systematically on this question; Briggs (1938) comes closest. This corpus tests several claims made in the literature:

Method and caveats

  • Corpus. 352 volumes: 299 colonial gazetteers, glossaries and census reports (1866–1935) and 53 revised gazetteers (1954–1989), plus the Bengal and Assam census reports for the Jugi study.
  • Coding. Language-model coders followed written rules. Every quotation was checked verbatim against the scan by script, and random samples (20, 16 and 14 passages) were reread by hand, with one minor miscode in each of the last two.
  • Counts are of passages, not opinions. One long glossary article (Rose, Thurston, Russell) yields many passages, and these weigh heavily in 1891–1935.
  • Judgement was coded from the writer’s own wording; opinions reported from others count as neutral. Religion and links to yoga were coded only where stated, so the Hindu share of “fakir” is a lower bound.
  • Coverage. The search terms are English spellings, so ascetics described without any of these words are missed. Madras is thin before 1935; Hyderabad and Kashmir are not covered.
