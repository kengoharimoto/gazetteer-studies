Yoga, yogis and Jogis in the gazetteers

What did “yoga”, “yogi” and “Jogi” mean to the officials who wrote the district gazetteers, caste glossaries and census reports? Did that change after independence? We coded 3,558 passages from about 350 volumes to find out.

Regions: Bombay and Sind, Madras, United Provinces, Bengal–Bihar–Orissa, Punjab, Central Provinces, Mysore and Kanara, Rajputana; the revised state gazetteers. Every quotation below was checked verbatim against the scanned text.

The answer in brief

  1. The colonial word was Jogi, not yogi. “Jogi” occurs 11–22 times per million words; “yoga” 1–2 times. In the coded passages the Jogi is a caste, a member of a Nāth order, a local saint or a beggar. Yoga as a philosophy or practice is 4–5% of the colonial passages.
  2. The writers themselves separate the two. Yoga is Patañjali’s school, learned and respectable. The Jogi of the district is its degenerate namesake.
  3. The district record is neutral, not hostile. The writer’s own judgement is neutral in 94% of the colonial passages. Contempt is concentrated in the criminal-caste literature and in a few quoted authorities.
  4. The spectacle-yogi of the travel books is almost absent. Ascetic practice is rarely described at all, and the raised arm and the bed of nails nearly never.
  5. The revaluation of “yogi” came from below. Between 1891 and 1931 the Jugi weavers of Bengal and Assam campaigned to be called Nath, Jogi and finally Yogi, as a claim to ascetic descent.
  6. After independence, yoga is respectable but still scholarly. Yoga as exercise first appears in the revised gazetteers of the 1950s–60s.

Who the Jogi was

In each period we coded every passage containing yoga, yogi, Jogi, Jugi, Kānphaṭa or Gorakhnāth, or a practice term such as samādhi or āsana. The chart shows what the term referred to. Passages where it was only a name, an astronomical unit or OCR noise are left out.

The balance differs by region. Before 1935 the Jogi was mainly a caste in Bengal, Madras, the Central Provinces and the UP. He was mainly a member of a Nāth order or a saint in the Punjab, home of the Tilla monastery, and in Mysore and Kanara. In Bombay he was as often a beggar or performer.

Two words, kept apart

The officials did not confuse the yogi of the books with the Jogi of the district. Several say outright that the living Jogi bears the name of a philosophy he no longer represents.

Yoga: the school
In its literal and much-respected sense, Yogi means a follower of Patanjali’s Yoga school who is devoted to divine meditation.Bombay Gazetteer IX-1, 1901
The term Jogi is derived from the Sanskrit yoga, union, and means a follower of the Yoga or Patanjal school of philosophy.Bombay Census 1911; Enthoven, Tribes and Castes of Bombay II, 1922
Jogi: the people
Used more precisely, it indicates a low class of people who make silk thread, string ornaments and sell strings to be worn as charms round the neck or waist. Many of them are strolling beggars.Gait, Census of Bengal 1901, para. 787
First are the Jogis proper, a regular religious order of Hindus, which includes both the Aughar Jogis and the Kanphatta Jogi ascetics.Rose, Glossary II, 1911. His second class is the low-caste Jogis, whom he calls rascally beggars.

The same split appears in Atkinson’s NWP Gazetteer (1879): “The Jogi is, strictly speaking, a Shaiva, and a follower of the Yoga, or philosophical school of Patanjali”. The same entry then describes Jogis as mountebanks. It appears again in the NWP and Oudh census of 1891, where Jogis “derive the name from a Yoga school of philosophy”. Yoga as something practised today is almost always meditation or trance. Before 1935 no passage treats posture as exercise.

Where the hostility is

Modern scholarship (Singleton, White, De Michelis) describes a colonial yogi lumped with the fakir and despised. The district record is calmer. Of 735 colonial passages on yoga and Jogis, the writer’s own judgement is negative in 27. Those 27 cluster in three places:

  • The criminal-caste literature. Thurston on the Telugu Jogis (1909), the Central Provinces gazetteers (1907, 1909), the Etah police circle (1911).
  • Rose’s Punjab glossary, on the Rawals and Muhammadan Jogis.
  • A single authority, quoted twice. L. D. Barnett wrote that “the Yoga system in its practical side is largely based upon vulgar ideas of magic and Shamanism, and hence many of its professors have always been vulgar charlatans”. The Damoh gazetteer quoted it in 1911, and the revised Amravati gazetteer reprinted it unchanged in 1968.

The same holds for the other words for ascetics. Among about 1,250 fakir and sādhu passages and about 880 sannyāsī, gosāīn and bairāgī passages, negative judgements are 5% and 3%.

Fakir, sādhu and the orders

  • “Fakir” was not a Muslim word. Where the religion is stated, Hindu or Sikh ascetics make up 29% of “fakir” passages in 1866–1890 (41 against 99 Muslim) and 24% in 1891–1935 (90 against 292). Half of all colonial “fakir” passages name no religion: fakirs are simply the mendicants who are fed, endowed or counted. The census filed Jogis under “Faqir”.
  • “Sādhu” is rare before independence (113 colonial passages against 974 for fakir). Rajputana is the exception: its census treated “the ascetic caste of Sadhus” as a caste, with tables of literacy and blindness. After 1954 “sādhu” becomes the usual word and “fakir” narrows to Muslims.
  • Practice is rarely described. Only 5% of fakir and sādhu passages and 10% of order passages describe any practice. Most of these are Sufi practices (dhikr, the forty-day chilla, ecstatic ḥāl) or saints’ legends. The bed of points appears once, in a medical officer’s neutral account of Dādūpanthī sādhus (Adams, Western Rajputana States, 1899).
  • The orders are institutions and estates. In 1891–1935 a fifth of sannyāsī, gosāīn and bairāgī passages describe married householders: zamindars, money-lenders, an Atīth caste. Only 45 of 883 connect the orders with yoga or Jogis, nearly always through census classification. In the NWP and Oudh census of 1891, “the words Goshain, Bairagi, Jogi, Saniyasi were apparently used by a considerable proportion of the enumerators as interchangeable”.
  • The armed ascetic is history. Fighting Gosains, Nāgās and Sannyāsī raiders appear 72 times in the past tense and twice in the present. This bears out Pinch’s account of the demilitarised ascetic, at district level.

The Jugis who became Yogis

The weaving caste called Jugi or Jogi in eastern Bengal and Assam turned “yogi” from a label of low status into a claim to rank. The census reports record each step.

1872–1877
Counted with the Patuas; in Hunter’s Statistical Account a despised weaving caste, “Jogi or Jugi”.
1891
Risley: legends of descent from Siddhas and Yoginīs, or from Nātha ascetics and Brahman widows. “In the Presidency districts the Jugis are assuming the sacred thread en masse.”
1901
“The Jogis strongly objected to the ordinary spelling of their caste name (Jugi) which is popularly derived from Jungi.” They call themselves Nath and their wives Debi. Gait adopts “Jogi” but rejects the Brahman claim.Census of Bengal 1901, para. 613, p. 381
1910–1911
District gazetteers: “Most Jugis call themselves Nath”; “the Jugis, or Jogis as they prefer to be called”. The 1911 census lists the name “Yogi” among the names claimed.Census of Bengal 1911, para. 828, p. 442
1921
Bengal: a Brahman enumerator “would rather cut off his hand than write down a Jugi as Jogi and his wife with the title of Debya like a Brahman woman”. Assam adopts Yogi as the caste name. The leaders chose the spelling “to emphasise their ascetic origin”.Census of Bengal 1921, p. 349; Census of Assam 1921, ch. XI
1931
The caste claims “Yogi” again. Its priests ask to be recorded “merely as Brahmans”, and are refused. Bengal keeps the heading “Jogi and Jugi” (384,634 persons).Census of Bengal 1931, pp. 428, 467–468

These dates are earlier than those in the current literature. The objection to “Jungi” belongs to 1901, not 1911. The 1931 Brahman claim was the priests’, not the whole caste’s. The claim rested on lineage (Gorakhnāth, the Nāth ascetics, seated burial), never on bodily practice.

After independence

In the revised gazetteers (1954–1989) the Jogi caste and the mendicant largely disappear. Yoga appears in three ways:

  • In Sanskrit learning: “Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vedanta” in college curricula, and Patañjali manuscripts in private libraries.
  • Through Vivekananda, Aurobindo and the Ramakrishna Mission, always respectfully. The earliest such account is colonial: the Howrah gazetteer of 1909 says Vivekananda “travelled in America, expounding the doctrines of yoga and Vedântism”.
  • In a few institutions, and only here as exercise: Kaivalyadhāma’s “Physical Culture and Yoga School” at Lonavla (Poona 1954). The Alwar glossary (1968) defines “Hath-yogi” as “a person following Yoga as a means of physical cultures”.

What this adds to the literature

Reading the sources with care

  • Counts are of passages, not of opinions. A single glossary article (Rose, Thurston, Russell) yields many passages and weighs heavily in 1891–1935.
  • Attitude was coded from the writer’s own wording. Opinions reported from others count as neutral.
  • Religion, and links to yoga, were coded only where the passage states them, so the Hindu share of “fakir” is a lower bound.
  • The search terms are English spellings. Passages that describe ascetics without any of the words searched for are not captured. Madras is thin before 1935, and Hyderabad and Kashmir are not covered.
  • Coding was done by language-model coders under written rules. Every quotation was checked verbatim by script. Random samples (20, 16 and 14 passages) were reread by hand, with one minor miscode in each of the last two.
