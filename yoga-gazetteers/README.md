# Yoga, yogis and Jogis in the gazetteers, 1866–1989

What did "yoga", "yogi" and "Jogi" mean to the people who wrote the colonial district gazetteers, caste glossaries
and census reports, and did that change by the time of the revised state gazetteers after independence?

## Answer in brief

1. **In the colonial texts (1866–1935) the yogi is not usually a yogi in the modern sense.**
   - The word the district writers actually used is **Jogi**: about 11–22 hits per million words.
   - "Yoga" appears 1–2 times per million words, and "yogi" 1.5–2.5 times.
   - In the coded passages, Jogi is overwhelmingly:
     - a **caste** (128 of 286 relevant passages in 1866–1890; 86 of 378 in 1891–1935);
     - a **member of an ascetic order**, chiefly the Kānphaṭa or Gorakhnāthī Nāths (89 and 155);
     - a **local saint** or a shrine (31 and 70);
     - a **beggar or performer** (25 and 29).
   - Yoga as a philosophy or a practice accounts for 12 of 286 and 21 of 378 passages.
2. **The writers keep the yogi of the books apart from the Jogi of the district.**
   - "Yoga" means **Patañjali's philosophical school**: a learned system, treated with respect.
   - The living Jogi is its degenerate namesake. Several writers say so outright:
     - "The Jogi is, strictly speaking, a Shaiva, and a follower of the Yoga, or philosophical school of Patanjali"
       (Atkinson, NWP Gazetteer V, 1879; P00637). The same entry then describes Jogis as mountebanks and mendicants.
     - "In its literal and much-respected sense, Yogi means a follower of Patanjali's Yoga school who is devoted to
       divine meditation" (Bombay Gazetteer IX-1, 1901; P00284). This is set against the vagrant Jogi.
     - "Jogis … derive the name from a Yoga school of philosophy" (NWP and Oudh Census Report, 1891; P00413). "Many"
       of them are then called mountebanks and fortune-tellers.
     - Rose's *Glossary* (1911; P01009) has two classes: "the Jogis proper, a regular religious order", against
       low-caste Jogis who are rascally beggars.
3. **Hostility is concentrated, not general.**
   - The writer's own judgement is neutral in 94% of the colonial passages: 621 of 664.
   - Negative judgements (24) cluster in three places:
     - the **criminal-caste and vagrancy literature**: Thurston on the Telugu Jogis (1909); the CP gazetteers
       (1907, 1909); the Etah police circle (1911);
     - **Rose's Punjab glossary** (1911–19), on the Rawal and Muhammadan Jogis;
     - comments on **Nāth institutions**, such as the Tilla monastery, "not a very estimable body" (1883; P01112).
   - A strongly negative statement about yoga itself is rare. The most quoted one comes from L. D. Barnett: "the Yoga
     system in its practical side is largely based upon vulgar ideas of magic and Shamanism, and hence many of its
     professors have always been vulgar charlatans" (Damoh, CP, 1911; P00166).
   - That sentence was **copied unchanged into the revised Amravati gazetteer of 1968** (P01260).
4. **Jogi as Muslim faqir.**
   - The census put Jogis under the heading "Faqirs": at least eight UP district gazetteers of 1903–11 say so, and
     Punjab 1891 has "Jogi & Rawal" under Faqir.
   - Jogi and faqir appear together in 48 colonial passages, all in the UP, the Punjab and the CP.
   - The glossaries describe **Muhammadan Jogis** (Rawals). The colonial "Jogi" category was not even confined to
     Hindus.
5. **Bengal: weavers who claimed the name.**
   - In Bengal and Assam the Jogi or Jugi is a **weaving caste**: Hunter 1875–77, ten districts; the Bengal District
     Gazetteers 1909–12.
   - By 1910–11 the gazetteers record its claims to status:
     - "Most Jugis call themselves Nath, and those who are educated Pandit-Nath" (BG17, 1910; P00020);
     - "The Jugis, or Jogis as they prefer to be called … a well educated and enterprising race" (Noakhali, 1911;
       P00015–16). This is the only positive colonial passage on a Jogi caste. The other eight positive colonial
       passages concern Gorakhnāth (a learned author), respected Kānphaṭa hermits, and ancient yogis of the caves.
   - The census reports show a campaign behind this, older than the literature's dates (`census/README.md`):
     - 1891: the thread and Brahman-descent legends (Risley);
     - 1901: objection to the spelling "Jugi" (Gait, Bengal);
     - 1911: the name "Yogi" formally claimed (O'Malley, Bengal);
     - 1921, Bengal: a Brahman enumerator "would rather cut off his hand than write down a Jugi as Jogi and his wife
       with the title of Debya like a Brahman woman" (Thompson);
     - 1921: "Yogi" adopted as the tabulated caste name, in Assam only, "to emphasise their ascetic origin" (Lloyd);
     - 1931: the caste's priests ask to be recorded as Brahmans, and are refused (Porter, Bengal).

     "Yogi" was turned from a mendicant's or weaver's label into a claim to ascetic descent.
   - In the same years the Malabar "Yogi Gurukkals" appear as a low but learned caste of exorcists and
     schoolmasters (Thurston 1909; P00406).
6. **Yoga as a bodily discipline is almost absent before independence.**
   - Before 1935 there is no passage on āsana as exercise or health.
   - "Practice of yoga" means meditation or trance: Dayānanda's yoga vidyā (UP07 1911; RUS1 1916), the Bairāgī's
     yoga-daṇḍa (Thurston 1909), prāṇāyāma within the Brahman sandhyā (Thurston 1909).
   - The modern reformers appear in three places:
     - the **Theosophical Society**: 13 G2 passages, all institutional notices;
     - Keshub Sen's "relapse" into "the mysticism of the Yogis" (Russell & Hira Lal, 1916; P00131);
     - a single district account of **Vivekananda**, in the Howrah gazetteer, because the Ramakrishna Mission had its
       headquarters at Belur in that district (O'Malley 1909; P00031–36). He "travelled in America, expounding the
       doctrines of yoga and Vedântism", and "the spirit of Vivekananda may stand for the spirit of the new era in
       India". This is the earliest passage in the corpus where yoga is something exported and modern.
7. **After independence, yoga is respectable but still mostly scholarly or spiritual.**
   - In the revised gazetteers (1954–89) the Jogi caste and the mendicant largely disappear (19 of 194 passages).
     Yoga appears in three ways:
     - in **Sanskrit curricula and manuscripts**: "Sankhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vedanta" (P01517, P01339);
     - through **Vivekananda, Aurobindo and the Ramakrishna Mission**, positively (21 positive judgements);
     - in a few **institutions**: Kaivalyadhāma's "Physical Culture and Yoga School" and "College of Yoga" at Lonavla
       (Poona 1954; P01316, P01322), and the Śivayoga Mandira (Bijapur 1959; P01391).
   - The many "physical culture" clubs listed in these volumes are vyāyāmaśālās and gymnasia, not yoga.
   - The positive revaluation is therefore visible, but yoga as mass exercise is not yet a gazetteer subject.

These findings support the broad scholarly narrative of a disreputable "Jogi" replaced by a respectable "yoga"
(literature: `results/literature.md`). They refine it in four ways:
- the colonial district record is **neutral and taxonomic** rather than hostile;
- its main Jogi is a **caste**, not a fakir-performer;
- the split between textual yoga and the living Jogi is **made explicitly by the writers themselves**, through the
  spelling yogi/Jogi;
- the hostile stereotype lives on mainly in the **criminal-tribes literature and in Barnett's sentence**, which
  survived into 1968.

## Corpus

The OCR downloaded for `../gazetteer-vedanta/` was reused; nothing new was fetched. Duplicate scans were dropped: three
Cornell scans of Atkinson are copies of AT01, AT02 and AT11 by text overlap (`scripts/merge.py`).

|---|---|---|---|---|

| G2 | 1891–1935 | 165 | 21.2 M | UP (Nevill), Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, CP, Punjab and Madras district gazetteers; Rose, Russell & Hira Lal, Thurston; census reports (NWP 1891, Madras, Bombay 1901) |

Regional gaps: Bombay has almost no G2 volume; Madras G1 and G2 are thin; there is no Assam, Sind, Rajputana,
Hyderabad or Kashmir.

## Method

1. `scripts/corpus.py`: catalogue of the OCR files with region, year and generation (`data/corpus.tsv`).
2. `scripts/lexicon.py`: counts of 13 term families per volume (`results/term_counts.tsv`) and keyword-in-context
   lines for the core families (`results/kwic.tsv`).
3. `scripts/passages.py`: nearby hits merged into 1,592 passages; 254 index or table passages set aside; the rest
   split into nine batches (`data/batches/`).
4. Coding by nine agents following `data/CODING_INSTRUCTIONS.md`:
   - **sense** (PHIL, DISCIPLINE, MODERN, ASCETIC, MENDICANT, CASTE, SAINT, or irrelevant);
   - the **writer's attitude**;
   - **tags**;
   - a **verbatim quote** for each passage.
5. `scripts/merge.py`:
   - merges the batches (`results/coded_all.tsv`, 1,338 rows);
   - checks every quote against its passage (1,002 of 1,004 verbatim; the two failures are a doubled quotation mark
     and an OCR fragment);
   - drops duplicate-scan rows and repeats within a generation (68 rows);
   - flags revised-gazetteer passages copied from colonial ones (`copy_of`);
   - writes `results/sense_by_gen.tsv` and `results/tags_by_gen.tsv`.
6. Check: 20 randomly drawn coded passages were reread against the text, and all 20 codes were acceptable.

Passage ids (P00001…) refer to `data/passages.tsv` and `results/coded_all.tsv`, where the full context, volume and
year are given. Page numbers are given where the OCR shows a folio.

## Caveats

- **Counts are of passages, not of opinions.** One glossary article on Jogis yields many passages, so Rose
  (Punjab) and Thurston (Madras) weigh heavily in G2.
- **Attitude is coded conservatively**, from the writer's own wording. Reported opinions ("the people revere him")
  are neutral.
- **The term list is English-spelling based** (yoga, yogi, jogi, jugi, Kanphata, Gorakhnath, samādhi, āsana,
  prāṇāyāma …). Passages that describe yogic practice without these words (e.g. "an ascetic sitting with his arm
  raised") are not captured. *Fakir*, *sannyāsī*, *gosāīn*, *bairāgī* and *sādhu* were counted but not coded.
