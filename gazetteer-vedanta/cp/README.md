# Central Provinces and Berar: Daśanāmīs, 1866–1916

Part of `../` (with Bombay, Madras, the North, Bengal and the Punjab). Śaṅkara's following appears here, as
in the north and east, as the Daśanāmī orders: Gosains, Gosavis (Marathi, Berar), Sannyasis.

## 1. Numbers

- **Province, 1911 (Russell):** Gosains "a little over 40,000", distributed over all districts. Bairagis about
  38,000; Jogis about 15,000. This is the same order of magnitude as the United Provinces (108,320 Daśanāmī
  Goshains in a population five times larger). The Daśanāmīs are relatively denser in the CP than in the UP,
  and far denser than in the Punjab.
- **District figures** are rare:
  - Nagpur 6,203 Gosains (Grant, 1866 census).
  - Buldana 1,900 Gosavis against 400 Bairagis and 500 Jogis (1901).
  - Bilaspur "about 10,000 Bairagis and Gosains" (1901).
  - Gosain villages: Nagpur 50–70; Jubbulpore 30; Narsinghpur 42 (36 in 1863–64).
- **The comparison orders are as numerous.** Bairagis number about 38,000 for the province, and they hold more
  land in Chhattisgarh (156 villages in Bilaspur). Kabirpanthis and Satnamis are the large popular sects of
  Chhattisgarh (Bilaspur Kabirpanthis 99,260).

## 2. What they were

1. **Maratha-period grantees, warlords and revenue farmers.** This is the most distinctive CP pattern.
   - Bheraghat (Jubbulpore) was granted by Raghuji Bhonsla to Mahant Hardeo Puri Gosain; still held by his
     disciples (Mahant Jagjiwanpuri).
   - Seoni: Kharak Bharti Gosain, a Bania by birth and a former moneylender, farmed the Seoni government from
     Raghuji Bhonsla in 1808 for 3 lakhs a year and built a fort at Adegaon. His chela succeeded him.
   - Raipur: the Dharampuri estate descends from an akhāṛā founded by Bichipuri. He lent money for villages,
     kept 500 Nāgās for the Raja of Nagpur, and collected tribute by force. Russell adds that the Nāgās
     invaded Bastar and brought the goddess Danteshwari to Deopur. By 1909 the line is a married mahant with
     40 villages, and "the Akhara has disappeared".
   - Other episodes:
     - Kalyan Gir Gosain, a Maratha deputy in Chhattisgarh (1745).
     - A Gosain force killed the Makrai Raja at Handia (1761).
     - Raghobharti Gosain impersonated Appa Sahib in Berar (1848).
2. **Narmada maṭhs.**
   - Gondagaon (Hoshangabad): a Gosain math at the triple confluence, endowed with villages by Sindhia, Holkar
     and the Saoligarh Raja.
   - Omkareshwar/Mandhata (Nimar): Daryao Nath Gosain is the sole worshipper in 1165 in the local account; his
     disciples still hold lands.
   - Mendha (Bhandara): the Alon Baba math of the Giri order, which had 7–8 villages and now has one.
   - Russell: those initiated on the Narmada throw their hair into the river, and he gives rules for the
     Narmada parikrama.
3. **Berar: Gosains as village priests.**
   - Village pujaris are "more frequently Gosains" than Brahmans. Their temple-and-tomb complexes are called
     maths.
   - They recognise the Mahant of Mahur (Nizam's Dominions) as their head, "a Gosawi religious dignitary of
     great position".
   - Naked Gosains travelled in bodies of up to 500.
4. **The caste (Russell, 1916).**
   - The ten names and four seats are given, none of the seats in the CP. Giri, Puri and Bharthi are commonest.
   - Admission is open to any caste from which a Brahman takes water, and locally even to Gonds and Kols.
   - Most are married. All Giris marry. They eat meat and drink liquor, and Brahmans do not take water from
     them.
   - They are gurus of the middle and lower castes and credited with converting tribes.
   - "Many have become landholders through grants … or through moneylending", and are "harsh landlords and
     grasping creditors".
   - Celibacy is the rule in maths, where property passes to a chela, but keeping women is "very common".

## 3. Findings in relation to the other regions

1. **The CP links the UP and Bombay patterns.**
   - In the Hindi-speaking north (Saugor to Jubbulpore, Narmada valley) and in Chhattisgarh, Daśanāmīs appear
     as in the UP: propertied Gosain mahants and householders.
   - In Berar, under the Marathi name Gosavi, they appear as village priests under a regional mahant (Mahur).
   - Berar is the Marathi side. It continues into the Bombay Deccan, where the Gazetteer recorded Gosavis as a
     caste but Śaṅkara's following as Smārta Brahmans under Saṅkeśvar or Śṛṅgeri.
2. **Maratha state-building used the orders.**
   - The Bhonsla state gave the Gosains land, revenue farms and military commands (Bheraghat, Seoni, Raipur's
     Nāgās).
   - It gave Bairagis the same (Lormi, and the Bairagi chiefs of Nandgaon and Chhuikhadan).
   - Colonial settlement then turned these grants into married family estates.
   - The parallels are the UP (Himmat Bahadur, Indargir) and Bengal (the Bodh Gayā farman).
3. **Two clear transformations by 1900–1910:**
   - from fighting akhāṛā to married zamindār family (Raipur: "the Akhara has disappeared");
   - from order to caste (Russell: "Gosain" now mostly means the married caste).
