# Rival ascetic orders in the north and centre: institutions and estates

This report covers the Bairāgīs (Rāmānandī and others), the Nāth/Kānphaṭā Jogīs, the Udāsīs and Nirmalas, the
Kabīrpanthīs, the Satnāmīs and the Aghorīs. It draws on the colonial volumes for the United Provinces (excluding
Benares, Fyzabad and Muttra), the Punjab and the Central Provinces. The coded rows are in `coded/N.tsv` (N001–N095,
95 rows, every quote checked verbatim against the OCR).

## Sources read

- **United Provinces (`north/`).** The volumes are:
  - Atkinson, *NWP* (1874–86), vols I–XIII except vol XIV (Benares). From vol VIII only the Allahabad part was read.
  - *Gazetteer of Oudh* (1877–78): the Fyzabad and Ajodhya entries were left aside.
  - Nevill's District Gazetteers (1903–11), 45 districts.

  The rows come mainly from Dehra Dun, Saharanpur, Gorakhpur, Basti, Gonda, Unao, Bara Banki, Allahabad, Meerut,
  Mainpuri, Aligarh, Jaunpur, Jalaun, Farrukhabad, Naini Tal and Atkinson's Himalayan volumes.
- **Punjab (`punjab/`).**
  - The 1883–84 district gazetteers (28 volumes) and the revised Part A volumes of 1895–1919 (21).
  - Rose's *Glossary*: vol II (1911, entries Bairagi and Jogi) and vol III (1914, entries Udasi and Nirmala). Vol I
    was searched only.
- **Central Provinces (`cp/`).**
  - Grant's *Gazetteer* (1870).
  - The District Gazetteers of 1905–11 (19 districts).
  - Russell and Hira Lal (1916): vol I (articles on Kabīrpanthī and Satnāmī), vol II (Aghori, Bairagi) and vol III
    (Gosain, Jogi; Gutenberg text, without page numbers).
- **Method.**
  - The order names and their OCR variants were searched in each volume, near words for land, maṭhs and
    conflict, and each relevant entry was then read in full.
  - Generic caste-list and census-table mentions are counted, volume by volume, in the `# generic` line at the end
    of `N.tsv`.

## What the texts show (with figures)

**1. Land: the rival orders held estates as large as the Daśanāmīs', and some much larger.**
- **The Bairāgī states and taluks of Chhattisgarh.** These are the largest ascetic holdings found.
  - Nandgaon: a Feudatory State of 560 villages, granted in 1723 to the Bhonsla's family priest, with tribute of
    about Rs 46,000 (Grant 1870) (N093). With Chhuikhadan it was a Bairāgī chiefship.
  - Russell says both states arose from loans to Gond zamindars. The Marathas then recognised them as zamindaris,
    and by 1916 they passed by primogeniture (N075).
  - Lormi: 103–104 villages (58,368 acres), granted in 1826 by the Raja of Nagpur to Baramdas Bairāgī. It passed to
    his chela and then to the chela's four sons, who quarrelled over the partition (N079, N083).
  - The celibate Nihang gaddī of Seorinarayan holds 6 revenue-free and 12 other villages (N080). In Raipur the
    Dudhadhari temple's mahant holds about 30 villages (N082).
  - Bairāgī villages by district: Bilaspur 156 of 2,242; Drug 41 (N081, N095).
- **Udāsīs (Nānakshāhīs) in Oudh.** Mahant Har Charan Dās of Maswāsī was a Nānakshāhī faqīr and an Oudh
  taluqdār.
  - Unao: 28 villages, acquired largely as zamindars put their villages under his protection against the
    chakladārs (N021).
  - Gonda: 17,215 acres, assessed at Rs 19,365 (N020).
  - He also held land in Bahraich, Kheri, Lucknow and Hardoi.
  - Gonda's table of holdings by caste: Goshains 34 villages, Nanakshahis 20, Bairagis 12 (N019).
- **Udāsīs at Dehra and Kankhal.**
  - The Guru Rām Rāi temple at Dehra is "the most important landed proprietor" of Dehra Dun. It holds Dehra itself
    (nominal revenue Rs 11,289) and five other revenue-free villages, all Garhwal and Gurkha grants, plus jāgīrs in
    Tehri (N006–N009, N033).
  - At Kankhal, Gokul Dās Udāsī holds 3,199 acres (Rs 4,412), next to Bhola Gir Goshain with 4,024 acres
    (Rs 4,818) (N003). In Jwalapur pargana the Udāsīs hold 4.6% of the land against the Goshains' 4.9% (N004).
- **Udāsī deras and langars in the Punjab.**
  - Dera Baba Nanak: Udāsī mahants since 1758, with a jāgīr of Rs 2,602 in perpetuity. The Tāhlī Sāhib jāgīr was cut
    from Rs 2,500 to Rs 1,000 (N055).
  - Bhuman Shah: "several fine villages" (N062).
  - Jaspal Bangar: a langar of about 800 acres, kept by a family of Udāsīs who marry (N060).
  - Sura: a jāgīr of Rs 800 earned by service in the Sikh rising of 1759 (N066).
  - Baksar (Meerut): a Nanakshahi colony with a revenue-free grant of 141 acres dating from Alamgir II (N047).
- **Nāth estates.**
  - Tilla Gorakhnāth, the Punjab's parent Jogī seat, held a jāgīr of Naugiran village under a sanad of Akbar. The
    jāgīr was resumed for over forty years after a mahant was accused of murdering his rival for the gaddī, and
    restored only recently. The Jogīs also hold pasture rights in the 26,000-acre Tilla rakh (N057, N058).
  - Koh Kirana's Sikh revenue grants were partly confirmed in perpetuity (N063). Kot Sarang's Pir draws Rs 715
    (N067).
  - Rohtak has 299 asthals with 659 ascetics on 2,725 acres. Half are Bairāgī; the Kānphaṭās of Bohar come next
    (N053).
  - The Gorakhnāth temple at Gorakhpur is described only as "in a very flourishing condition", living on offerings
    (N012, N013). Neither Gorakhpur volume gives villages for it.
  - Dehra Dun gives two villages to Gorakhnāth (N007).
- **Kabīrpanthī gaddīs hold jurisdiction, not land.**
  - Kawardha/Damakheda (the Dharam Dās line) claims 500,000 of India's 850,000 Kabīrpanthīs in 1901; Bilaspur
    alone has 99,260 (N077, N078).
  - Subordinate mahants buy letters of jurisdiction and keep the offerings.
  - The only land is Maghar's samādh, which has one revenue-free village (N017, N018).
- **Satnāmīs.**
  - Kotwa (Bara Banki): Jagjīvan Dās's hereditary mahants. Their family members hold land and marry into
    taluqdār families (N023–N025).
  - Bhandar (Raipur): Ghāsī Dās's grandsons partitioned their 4–5 villages and lost them through extravagance
    (N084).

**2. Organisation and its transformations.**
- **The same drift into married family estates as among the Gosāins.**
  - Lormi passes to sons; Nandgaon goes from adoption (1870) to primogeniture (1916).
  - Jaspal Bangar is a marrying Udāsī family.
  - In Karnal the gharbārī Bairāgīs form "a new caste" drawn from Jats (N050, N068).
- **Settlement as the mechanism.**
  - In Ludhiana the Regular Settlement returned each dharmsālā's sādh as owner of its land, revenue-free. Most then
    married, closed the dharmsālā and became "landed proprietors" (N059).
  - Punjab customary law holds that Jullundur Udāsīs have not given up their inheritance rights (N065).
- **Succession disputes went to the courts.**
  - Dera Baba Nanak: Bedi patrons against the mahants (civil court, 1857).
  - Kawardha: Dhīrajnām against Ugranām, decided by the Bombay High Court. The split left the subordinate gaddīs
    independent: in Jubbulpore followers "do not even know who the Kawardha Mahants are" (N087). The sources
    disagree on which claimant sat at Kudarmal and which at Damakheda.
  - Tilla lost its jāgīr over a murder for the gaddī (N058).

**3. Conflicts with the Gosāins.**
- **Hardwar, 1760.** At the Kumbh, Bairāgīs and Gosāins fought a pitched battle; the Bairāgīs were defeated with
  1,800 slain (N001, N010).
  - Russell gives the reason: the right to bathe first in the Ganges (Śiva's locks against Viṣṇu's foot). He says the
    seven Bairāgī akhāṛās "were instituted when the Bairagis had to fight with the Gosains" (N074, N076).
  - The Lasgarī akhāṛā's white sect-mark recalls a Gosāin surprise attack.
- **Jogīs against Saniāsīs.** Rose records a "deadly feud" between Jogīs and Saniāsīs: 500 Jogīs were beaten by 200–300
  Saniāsīs before Akbar, who sent ash-smeared soldiers to help the Saniāsīs (N069).
- **Fights among the Vaiṣṇava orders themselves.** The Bairāgī Nirbānīs fought the Nirmohīs and Digambarīs at the
  1906 Allahabad Kumbh, and a squadron of cavalry had to intervene (N027, N031).
- **A conversion story.** The Udāsī chapter Bhagat Bhagwān traces itself to a band of Sanniāsīs under Bhagatgir,
  converted by Nānak's grandson. It claims 370 gaddīs in Eastern India (N072).

**4. Aghorīs.** No estate is recorded. The only Aghorī institution found is a temple and monastery in the ruined fort
of Hariharpur (Jaunpur) (N041). Otherwise there are only Atkinson's and Russell's descriptions (N039, N092), and a
"stray specimen" in Kumaon (N038).

## Comparison with Śaṅkara's following in the same places

- **The rivals were the larger landholders in Chhattisgarh and Oudh.**
  - The CP study's largest Gosāin estates are Dharampuri's 40 villages (Raipur) and the Bheraghat and Seoni grants.
    Against them stand two Bairāgī Feudatory States (Nandgaon 560 villages), Lormi's 104 and Seorinarayan's 18.
  - The Maratha (Bhonsla) state endowed both orders the same way: Bairāgī grants of 1723, 1799 and 1826 (N088,
    N093, N079) beside the Gosāin ones. At Bham, Raghuji's camp is said to have held 5,000 Bairāgī houses (N091).
  - In Oudh the Daśanāmīs had nothing to match the Nānakshāhī taluqa of Maswāsī.
- **On the upper Ganges they were roughly equal.**
  - Kankhal and Jwalapur pair Gokul Dās Udāsī with Bhola Gir Goshain almost acre for acre.
  - The Allahabad akhāṛās are described as one type: urban, wealthy, banking. The Naga Gosāins of Daraganj sit beside
    the Udāsī Panchāyatī akhāṛās and the Nirmalas.
  - The Bairāgīs alone appear there as homeless (N028–N031).
- **In the Punjab the rival field dominates.** The Punjab README already shows that the Daśanāmīs thin out westward.
  - The institutions with land and royal sanads are Tilla, Koh Kirana, Bohar, Dera Baba Nanak, Bhuman Shah and the
    Ludhiana and Jalandhar dharmsālās.
  - Rohtak's 299 asthals include Gosāins only in fourth place.
  - Hisar pairs the Kānphaṭā seats at Bohar and Nohar with the Daśanāmī gurus at Kharak and Bilak (N064).
- **A division of village ritual labour.** Karnal's thakurdwaras go to Brahmans and Bairāgīs, its shivālās to Gosāins
  or Kānphaṭā Jogīs (N052). In the Himalaya Atkinson sets the Vaiṣṇava temples with Bairāgīs (whom he ties to
  Śrīraṅgam) and the Bhairava temples with Kānphaṭās beside the Daśanāmī Joshimath (N034–N036).
- **The same social form.** Bairāgīs, Udāsīs and Nāths show the pattern the study found for the Gosāins:
  - chela succession turning into married family estates;
  - mahants acting as moneylenders and landlords (Bilaspur explicitly derives both orders' estates from temple
    surpluses invested in land and loans, N081);
  - settlement and the courts fixing private title.

  What sets the rivals apart:
  - Two Bairāgī lines reached princely status (Nandgaon, Chhuikhadan).
  - The Kabīrpanthī and Satnāmī gaddīs rested on huge low-caste lay followings rather than land. In the Satnāmī case
    this came close to open conflict with the caste order: Balak Dās was murdered in 1860 (N085).

## Limits

- **Coverage.**
  - Benares, Fyzabad (Ayodhya) and Muttra are excluded, so the main Rāmānandī and Aghorī centres (Ayodhya, Kina Ram)
    are missing here.
  - The Feudatory States gazetteer of Chhattisgarh is not among the downloads. Chhuikhadan's entry in Grant is
    illegible in the OCR, so its estate details are not coded.
  - Nāth and Kabīrpanthī land is probably under-recorded: the district volumes describe these gaddīs by their
    followings and fairs, not their villages.
- **Files and labels.**
  - Several files are mislabelled in the selection lists: `dli.ministry.07867` (labelled Nimar) is Balaghat;
    `dli.ministry.07863` (labelled Nagpur) is a Bombay (Thana) volume; Basti DG is dated 1801.
  - `glossaryoftribes03rose` (labelled ROSE3) is Rose vol II. Rose vol III is `in.ernet.dli.2015.69674`.
- **Pages.** Page numbers come from running heads near the passage and can be off by one. Russell vol III (Gutenberg)
  has no pages.
- **Gaps in the reading.**
  - Rose vol I (shrine surveys in Karnal and elsewhere) was only searched, not coded. It has many small Bairāgī
    asthals and Udāsī-managed shrines.
  - Russell's Jogi article was read for institutions only; it has none.
- **Uncertain attribution.** The order is not stated in some rows (Mahant Dharam Dās of Kydganj, N032; Nanakmata,
  N037), and these are marked low or medium confidence.
